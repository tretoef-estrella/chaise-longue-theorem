# THE CHAISE LONGUE THEOREM
## Conjecture 1.2 of Degtyarev–Shimada for the Fermat varieties of every odd degree in every even dimension, and the integral Hodge conjecture for those whose degree is prime or prime to `(n+2)!`

**Rafael Amichis Luengo**

Madrid, Spain · tretoef@gmail.com · 1 October 2026 · version 9

---
## Abstract

Let `X ⊂ P^{n+1}` be the complex Fermat variety `z_0^m + ⋯ + z_{n+1}^m = 0` of even dimension `n = 2k` and degree `m ≥ 3`, and let `L(X) ⊆ H_n(X; Z)` be the subgroup generated by the classes of its standard `k`-dimensional linear subspaces. Degtyarev and Shimada conjectured that `H_n(X; Z)/L(X)` is torsion free [DS, Conjecture 1.2]. **We prove this conjecture for every odd degree `m`, in every even dimension.** In algebraic form, we prove with no topology that the group `Z[G]/(ψ_J)` of [DS] is free abelian for every odd `m`; the topology of [DS] identifies its torsion with that of `H_n(X; Z)/L(X)`. This algebraic statement has also been checked by machine: a complete proof in Lean 4 with Mathlib, with no gap and no axiom beyond the three standard ones (§12.8). Combined with the classical description of the rational Hodge classes of `X` (Shioda–Katsura, Shioda, Ran) and with Aoki's determination of the degrees in which every Hodge class is of «pair type», this gives **the integral Hodge conjecture for `X` whenever `m` is an odd prime or every prime factor of `m` exceeds `n + 2`**: in these degrees every integral Hodge class of middle degree is an integral combination of classes of standard linear subspaces. For every other odd `m` the standard subspaces span a primitive sublattice of the integral Hodge classes, with a free complement whose rank is the number of Hodge characters of `X` that are not of pair type (in closed form for `m = 9`). As a further consequence, the linear subspaces that [DS] exhibit on the partial Fermat varieties generate primitive sublattices, a statement that [DS] had proved conditionally on Conjecture 1.2 (these are the `(2s+1)!!·m^{d+1}` subspaces exhibited in [DS]; we do not claim that they are all the linear subspaces).

By the reduction of [DS] to commutative algebra, the conjecture at `m` is a dimension count over `F_p` for each prime `p` dividing `m`. For `m = q = p^v` it reads

`dim_{F_p} (D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)·C = Q_k(q)`,   `C = F_p[y_1, …, y_{2k+1}]/(y_1^{q−1}, …, y_{2k+1}^{q−1})`,

where `J` runs over the `(2k+1)!!` perfect matchings of `{0, …, 2k+1}`, `D_J` is the product, over the pairs `{a,b}` of `J` avoiding `0`, of `D(y_a, y_b) = (y_b^{q−1} − y_a^{q−1})/(y_a + y_b)`, and `Q_k(q) = (2k+2)!·[x^{2k+2}] I_0(2x)^{(q−1)/2}`. We prove this count (Theorem B) with equality over every field and for every odd `q`, and with a free quotient over the integers. The proof peels off one variable at a time: the slices of the ideal are matched with the fibres of a finite point set, the bookkeeping collapses onto partitions under weak dominance, the possible values of one new coordinate always form a single chain, and Vandermonde divided differences are the only glue. For `m = q·r` with `p ∤ r` and `r > 1`, the group algebra over `F̄_p` splits over the colourings of the coordinates by `r`-th roots of unity; on each factor the ideal becomes a tensor product of blocks, which are instances of Theorem 5.3 (the generalisation of Theorem B to down-sets of partitions) and a new family of **bipartite ideals**, generated by the products `Π_i (x_i − z_{σ(i)})^{q−1}` over bijections `σ`. We prove the corresponding lower bound (Theorem C), which is an equality over `F̄_p` (Corollary 7.8), by the same method, with pairs of partitions under the product of two weak dominance orders. Both arguments use the full family of matchings, and they must: for some proper subfamilies the analogous statement is false (an exact finite computation, Fact 9.2).

We also prove, and keep separate, a different count (Theorem A): for every field of characteristic `≠ 2`, every odd `q` and every `n`, `dim F[x_1, …, x_n]/(e_1, e_3, e_5, …; x_1^q, …, x_n^q) = n!·[y^n] e^y I_0(2y)^{(q−1)/2}`. Theorem A does **not** imply the conjecture (§9). In characteristic `0` this count follows from a theorem of Kostant on the centralizer of a principal nilpotent element (Remark 8.4); what Theorem A adds is the same count in every odd characteristic, with no relation between `q` and the characteristic, and hence the absence of odd torsion over `Z`.

## 1. Introduction and main results

### 1.1 The conjecture
We follow [DS]. Let `n = 2k` with `k ≥ 0`, let `m ≥ 3`, and let `X ⊂ P^{n+1}` be the Fermat variety `z_0^m + ⋯ + z_{n+1}^m = 0`. All homology is with coefficients in `Z`.
- `𝒥` is the set of partitions of the index set `{0, 1, …, n+1}` into unordered pairs, written `J = [[j_0,k_0], …, [j_k,k_k]]` with `j_i < k_i` and `j_0 < j_1 < ⋯ < j_k`; in particular `j_0 = 0`. There are `|𝒥| = (2k+1)!!` of them.
- For `J ∈ 𝒥` and `β = (β_0, …, β_k)` with `β_i^m = −1`, the **standard `k`-space** `L_{J,β} ⊂ X` is given by `z_{k_i} = β_i z_{j_i}` (`i = 0, …, k`).
- For `∅ ≠ K ⊆ 𝒥`, `L_K(X) ⊆ H_n(X)` is the subgroup generated by the classes `[L_{J,β}]` with `J ∈ K`; `L(X) := L_𝒥(X)`.

**Conjecture 1.2 of [DS].** *The group `H_n(X)/L(X)` is torsion free.*

For `k = 1` the conjecture is a theorem of Degtyarev [De14]; for `m` prime to `6` it implies that the Picard group of the Fermat surface is generated by its lines. For `k ≥ 2` it has been confirmed by computer for `(n, m) = (4, m)` with `3 ≤ m ≤ 12`, and for `(6,3)`, `(6,4)`, `(6,5)`, `(8,3)` [DS, §5]. Aljovin, Movasati and Villaflor [AMV] compared the lattice of linear cycles with the lattice of Hodge cycles by computing elementary divisors, for the Fermat surfaces of degree `3 ≤ m ≤ 14` and the Fermat fourfolds of degree `3 ≤ m ≤ 6` [AMV, Table 1]. The conjecture is restated as open in [De15, Conjecture 4.4]. Before the present work we knew of no proof for any `k ≥ 2` beyond these cells; in particular it had not been decided for any odd degree that is not a prime power when `k ≥ 2` (the odd degrees in the table of [DS, §5] are prime powers). As [DS, §1] point out, for prime `m` the rational Hodge classes of `X` are spanned by the classes of the standard spaces, so that the conjecture would upgrade this to the *integral* Hodge classes; §10 carries this out, for prime `m` and for more degrees.

### 1.2 The main theorem
Put `N := 2k+2 = n+2`, and for odd `q ≥ 3` let

`Q_k(q) := N!·[x^N] I_0(2x)^{(q−1)/2}`,   `I_0(2x) = Σ_{b≥0} x^{2b}/(b!)^2`.

For odd `m`, `Q_k(m)` is the number `|Γ_𝒥|` of [DS, Definition 1.3] (Lemma 2.2), so that `rank L(X) = Q_k(m) + 1` by Theorem 0(c) below.

> **MAIN THEOREM.** *Let `m ≥ 3` be odd and `k ≥ 0`. Then `H_{2k}(X; Z)/L(X)` is torsion free for the Fermat variety `X` of degree `m` and dimension `2k`. Equivalently, `L(X)` is a primitive sublattice of `H_{2k}(X; Z)`, of rank `Q_k(m) + 1`.*

For `k = 0` the statement is trivial: `X` is a set of `m` points and `L(X) = H_0(X)`. From now on `k ≥ 1` whenever topology is involved.

The Main Theorem has an algebraic form, which is proved here with no topology at all (Corollary 7.7). Let `R := Z[t_1, …, t_{2k+1}]/(t_1^m − 1, …, t_{2k+1}^m − 1)` be the integral group ring of `G = (Z/m)^{2k+1}` and let `ψ_J ∈ R` be the generators of [DS], recalled in §2.

> **MAIN THEOREM′ (algebraic form).** *Let `m ≥ 3` be odd and `k ≥ 1`. Then `R/(ψ_J : J ∈ 𝒥)` is a free abelian group of rank `m^{2k+1} − Q_k(m)`. Equivalently, `dim_{F_p} F_p[G]/(ψ̄_J : J ∈ 𝒥) = m^{2k+1} − Q_k(m)` for every prime `p`.*

By [DS, Theorem 1.1(a)] (Theorem 0(a) below) the torsion of `R/(ψ_J : J ∈ 𝒥)` is that of `H_{2k}(X; Z)/L(X)`, so Main Theorem′ implies the Main Theorem; the rank of `L(X)` is [DS, Theorem 1.4]. These two statements are the only places where topology enters.

The proof has three parts.
- **§2 (the translation).** Using the results of [DS] collected in Theorem 0, torsion-freeness at `m` is equivalent to a dimension count over `F_p` for each prime `p | m` (Proposition 2.1); for `m = q = p^v` the count is **(S)** `dim_{F_p}(D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)C = Q_k(q)` (Proposition 2.5).
- **§5 (prime powers).** Theorem B below proves (S).
- **§6–§7 (degrees that are not prime powers).** For `m = q·r` with `q = p^v`, `p ∤ r`, `r > 1`, the count at `p` splits over the colourings of the coordinates by `r`-th roots of unity into a sum of products of block dimensions (Proposition 6.9). The blocks are instances of Theorem 5.3 and bipartite ideals, whose count is Theorem C (§7).

Sections 5–7 prove lower bounds for the dimensions of the ideals: Theorems 5.3 and 7.6. The matching upper bound is the inequality of Proposition 2.1, and it is algebraic too: it is [DS, Claim 4.3] (Theorem 0(b)), a count of points by the Chinese remainder theorem over a field of characteristic `0` (Appendix B, Step 6). So Main Theorem′ is proved without topology; topology enters only through Theorem 0(a) and 0(c), which identify the torsion of `R/(ψ_J)` with that of `H_{2k}(X; Z)/L(X)` and give the rank of `L(X)`. Theorems 5.9 and 5.9′, §4, §8 and §9 are not on the path of the proof; Corollary 7.8 is a consequence of it. Main Theorem′ has been proved again in the proof assistant Lean 4 (§12.8).

The cubic case `q = 3` has a shorter proof with an explicit basis given by ballot paths (§4, Theorem 4.1).

### 1.3 The integral Hodge conjecture
For a smooth complex projective variety `Y`, the integral Hodge conjecture in degree `2j` asks whether every class in `H^{2j}(Y; Z) ∩ H^{j,j}(Y)` is the class of an algebraic cycle with integer coefficients. It is false in general [AH], [Kol], and it remains open for many varieties for which the rational Hodge conjecture is known; for Fermat varieties it is the question studied in [AMV]. Write `Hdg(X) ⊆ H_n(X; Z)` for the group of integral Hodge classes of middle degree (§10.1).

> **COROLLARY H.** *Let `m ≥ 3` be odd, `k ≥ 1`, and let `X` be the Fermat variety of degree `m` and dimension `2k`.*
> - *(i) If `m` is prime, or if every prime factor of `m` is at least `2k + 3`, then `Hdg(X) = L(X)`: every integral Hodge class of middle degree is an integral linear combination of classes of standard `k`-spaces. In particular the integral Hodge conjecture holds for `X` in every degree.*
> - *(ii) For every odd `m`, `L(X)` is a primitive sublattice of `Hdg(X)` and `Hdg(X)/L(X)` is free of rank `|𝔅| − Q_k(m)`, where `𝔅` is the set of Hodge characters of §10.1. It is zero if and only if `m` is prime or every prime factor of `m` is at least `2k + 3`.*

For odd `m`, «every prime factor of `m` is at least `2k + 3`» is the same as `gcd(m, (n+2)!) = 1`, and also the same as the condition `gcd(m, (n+1)!) = 1` of [AMV, §1]. The proof is in §10. It combines the Main Theorem with the classical description of the rational Hodge classes of `X` by characters [SK], [Shi79], [Ran] ((H3) of §10.1), and with the determination, due to Aoki [Aok, Theorem A], of the degrees in which every Hodge character is of pair type; for prime `m` we give a short proof of this last fact (§10.1, (H4)). The new input is the primitivity of `L(X)`. The classical fact that the rational Hodge classes of pair type are spanned by linear subspaces [Ran, Theorem 4.9] is not used: over `Z` it follows from the Main Theorem.

*What was known.* For `k = 1` the integral Hodge conjecture is the Lefschetz `(1,1)` theorem, and (i) says that the lines generate the Néron–Severi group, which was known when `gcd(m, 6) = 1` [SSvL], [De14], and classically for the cubic surface; for odd `m` these are exactly the degrees of (i) at `k = 1`. For `(k, m) = (2, 3)` the integral Hodge conjecture itself follows from Voisin's theorem on cubic fourfolds [Voi, Theorem 2.11]; as [AMV, §1] note, the stronger statement `Hdg(X) = L(X)` does not follow from it. The cells `(2, 3)` and `(2, 5)` were verified by computer in [AMV]. In each prime cell where [DS, §5] confirmed the primitivity of `L(X)` by computer, namely `(k, m) = (2, 3), (2, 5), (2, 7), (2, 11), (3, 3), (3, 5), (4, 3)`, (i) already followed from that computation and the classical facts by the argument of §10.3; [AMV, §1] make this observation for the cells of their table. The author had also verified the primitivity of `L(X)` by computer, before the present proof, in the prime cells `(k, m) = (2, 13), (2, 17), (2, 19), (3, 7), (4, 5), (5, 3)` [Rep]; three of them, `(2, 13)`, `(3, 7)` and `(5, 3)`, are re-run in §12.1. What is new, for `k ≥ 2`, is the uniform statement: every other cell with `m` prime, and every cell of (i) with `m` not prime, for instance `(k, m) = (2, 49)`, `(2, 77)`, `(3, 121)`, `(3, 143)`; to our knowledge no cell of (i) with `m` not prime and `k ≥ 2` had been decided before. Part (ii) is new for `k ≥ 2`, to our knowledge; for `k = 1` it follows from [De14] and the rank `|𝔅| + 1` of (H3). Jumagulov [Jum] recently gave a computer-assisted proof of the *rational* Hodge conjecture for the Fermat fourfolds of odd degree at most `199`; that statement is rational, and complementary to ours.

### 1.4 Partial Fermat varieties
Let `0 ≤ s ≤ d`. Following [DS, §1], a **partial Fermat variety** is a smooth hypersurface `W_s ⊂ P^{2d+1}` given by `f_0(z_0, z_1) + f_1(z_2, z_3) + ⋯ + f_d(z_{2d}, z_{2d+1}) = 0`, where the `f_i` are binary forms of degree `m`, `f_0 = ⋯ = f_s = x^m + y^m`, and `f_{s+1}, …, f_d` are generic, pairwise distinct and distinct from `x^m + y^m`. [DS, §1] exhibit `(2s+1)!!·m^{d+1}` linear `d`-spaces on it.

> **COROLLARY W.** *Let `m ≥ 3` be odd and `0 ≤ s ≤ d`.*
> - *(i) The classes of the `(2s+1)!!·m^{d+1}` linear `d`-spaces of [DS, §1] on `W_s` generate a primitive sublattice of `H_{2d}(W_s; Z)`.*
> - *(ii) On the Fermat variety `X` of dimension `2d`, let `𝒥(s, d) ⊆ 𝒥` be the set of matchings that contain the pairs `{2i, 2i+1}` for `s < i ≤ d`. Then `L_{𝒥(s,d)}(X)` is primitive in `H_{2d}(X; Z)`, of rank `Q_s(m)(m−1)^{d−s} + 1`.*

[DS, Corollary 1.7] states (i) under the assumption that Conjecture 1.2 holds for the Fermat varieties of dimension `2s` and degree `m`, and proves it for `s = 0, 1`; [De15, §4.2–§4.3] reduces (i) and (ii) to the same assumption and proves them in the same cases (in [De15] the index is the dimension `2s`). The Main Theorem removes the assumption. The proof is in §11, where (ii) is proved directly.

### 1.5 The algebraic statements
Let `F` be a field and `q ≥ 3` odd. Put `C_F := F[y_1, …, y_{2k+1}]/(y_1^{q−1}, …, y_{2k+1}^{q−1})` and define `D(a, b)` and `D_J` as in the abstract: `D` is the polynomial of §2.3, which has integer coefficients.

> **THEOREM B.** *For every field `F`, every odd `q ≥ 3` and every `k ≥ 0`,*
>
> `dim_F (D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)·C_F = Q_k(q)`.
>
> *Moreover, with `C_Z := Z[y_1, …, y_{2k+1}]/(y_1^{q−1}, …, y_{2k+1}^{q−1})`, the group `C_Z/(D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)C_Z` is free abelian of rank `(q−1)^{2k+1} − Q_k(q)`, and the dimension of each graded piece of `(D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)C_F` does not depend on `F`.*

Theorem B is the root case of a more general statement about down-sets of partitions (Theorem 5.3 for `≥`; Theorems 5.9 and 5.9′ for `=` and for the integral statement). Neither the topology nor [DS] enters its proof; they enter only in the translation of §2. With Theorem B and Proposition 2.5, the Main Theorem follows for `m = p^v` (Corollary 5.4).

The degrees that are not prime powers need a second count, in two sets of variables `x_1, …, x_α` and `z_1, …, z_β` with the box `(x_i^q, z_l^q)`, for `q = p^v`: the **balanced** bipartite ideal `I^{bal}_a`, generated by `Π_{i=1}^{a}(x_i − z_{σ(i)})^{q−1}` over all `σ ∈ S_a` (`α = β = a`), and the **phantom** bipartite ideal `I^{ph}_a`, generated by the same products over all bijections from `a` of the `a + 1` variables `x_i` onto the `z_l` (`α = a + 1`, `β = a`). **Theorem C** (§7) says that their dimensions are at least the numbers of pairs `(ξ, η)` of tuples in a set of `q` elements, of lengths `(a, a)` and `(a+1, a)`, such that the multiset of `η` is equal to, respectively contained in, the multiset of `ξ`. Over `F̄_p` these are equalities (Corollary 7.8), and the dimensions do depend on the characteristic.

### 1.6 A different count: Theorem A
Write `E := (e_1, e_3, …, e_{2k+1}) ⊆ F_p[x_0, …, x_{2k+1}]` for the ideal of the odd elementary symmetric polynomials. Over an algebraic closure its zero set is the union of the linear spaces `x_a + x_b = 0` (`{a,b} ∈ J`), over all `J ∈ 𝒥`.

> **THEOREM A.** *Let `F` be a field of characteristic `≠ 2`, let `q ≥ 1` be odd and let `n ≥ 0`. Then `dim_F F[x_1, …, x_n]/(e_1, e_3, e_5, …; x_1^q, …, x_n^q) = n!·[y^n] e^y I_0(2y)^{(q−1)/2}`.*

With `F = F_p`, `q = p^v` and `n = N`, Theorem A says that `A_k(q) := dim F_p[x_0, …, x_{2k+1}]/(E + (x_i^q))` equals the number `P_k(q)` of `F_q`-points of the union of the matching spaces (Corollary 8.1). **This is not the Main Theorem, and it does not imply it.** Theorem A is a *sum* statement at the *odd* box `q` on the grid *with* `0`; the Main Theorem for `m = q` is equivalent to an *intersection* statement at the *even* box `q − 1` on the grid *without* `0` (Proposition 2.6), and §9 shows that the two are independent for families of matchings. The numbers differ as well: `P_2(3) = 141` against `Q_2(3) = 20`. In characteristic `0`, Theorem A is a consequence of a classical theorem of Kostant [Kos] (Remark 8.4); its new content is the statement in every characteristic `≠ 2`.

### 1.7 The idea of the proofs
Write the ideal of Theorem B as `V ⊆ C_{2k+1}` and the point set it should count as `Z ⊆ T^{2k+1}`, `T = F_q^×`. Peel off the variable `y_1`: the ideal splits into `q − 1` slices `W_0 ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ W_{q−2}`, ideals in the remaining variables, and the point set splits into `q − 1` layers according to how many values of the first coordinate complete a given tail. If every layer is the point set of an ideal contained in the matching slice, induction on the number of variables gives `dim V ≥ |Z|`. To make this work we enlarge the family: to every down-set `Λ` of partitions under weak dominance we attach an ideal `V_Λ`, generated by products of divided differences `D` and Vandermonde determinants `Δ`, and a point set `Z_Λ`, the tuples whose «unpaired part» has shape in `Λ`. Three facts then carry the induction. The number of completions of a tail depends only on the shape `μ` of the tail (P1). The `q − 1` possible new shapes form a chain for weak dominance, so the completions in `Λ` are an initial segment of the chain, and the layers are again down-sets (P2). And each generator of a layer can be lifted to the right slice by one of three explicit constructions, with no room to spare (P3). The full family of matchings enters only once, at the root: the down-set generated by the partition `(1)` has as ideal exactly the ideal of all the `D_J`.

For `m = q·r` with `q = p^v`, `p ∤ r` and `r > 1`, `t^m − 1 = Π_{ζ ∈ μ_r}(t − ζ)^q` over `F̄_p`, so the group algebra is a product of local rings, one for each colouring `c` of the coordinates by `r`-th roots of unity. On the factor of `c`, the generator `ψ_J` vanishes unless every pair of `J` has colours `ζ, ζ^{−1}`; the surviving generators are pure tensors along the colour classes, and the ideal is a tensor product of one ideal per class of colour `1` and one per pair of inverse colours. The class of colour `1` is an instance of Theorem 5.3. A pair of inverse colours becomes, after the change of variables `x = ζ^{−1}t − 1`, `z = (ζt)^{−1} − 1`, a bipartite ideal, and the colour of the index `0`, which is determined by the others, decides whether it is balanced or phantom. The count of the points factors over the colourings in the same way. For the bipartite ideals, the tail of a point now has two residues, one for each set of variables, and its shape is a pair of partitions: adding a value to the `x`-side removes a box from the `z`-residue, adds a box to the `x`-residue, or starts a new part, and these `q` options again form a chain, now in the product of two weak dominance orders. The proof of Theorem B then goes through with the box `q` in place of `q − 1` and the binomial coefficients `C(q − 1, t) ≡ (−1)^t` in place of the signs of `D`.

### 1.8 A note on the history
For several months the project that produced this paper took the count `A_k(q) = P_k(q)` of Theorem A to be the algebraic form of Conjecture 1.2. Two earlier notes of the author, on the cases `k = 2` (*The Sofa Theorem* [Sofa]) and `k = 3` (*The Hammock Theorem* [Ham]), were written under that identification and stated that they established Conjecture 1.2 in those dimensions. On 23 September 2026, re-deriving the reduction from the original [DS], we found that the identification had never been proved, and that it is false for families of matchings (§9). The conclusions of those notes about Conjecture 1.2 in dimensions 4 and 6 are nevertheless true: for odd `m` they are the cases `k = 2, 3` of the Main Theorem, proved here by a different route. The previous version of this paper proved the Main Theorem for odd prime powers and stated the colour reduction for the degrees that are not prime powers as an open problem; the reduction and the bipartite count (§6–§7) complete it. The present version (v8) adds the machine-checked proof of Main Theorem′ (§12.8), records that the repository [Rep] is public, states the status of the *Double Ladder* theorem more precisely (Remark 10.2), and adds one clause to the proof of Lemma 6.8; no statement and no proof of the previous version changes. Version 9 (the present one) corrects the attribution of Theorem A: in characteristic `0` it follows from a theorem of Kostant, which the earlier versions did not cite (Remark 8.4). It also records that the *Watermark* and *Double Ladder* theorems are now proved for every prime and verified in Lean [WDL] (Remark 10.2). Again no statement and no proof changes.

## 2. The translation: Conjecture 1.2 as a dimension count

This section is written from the original [DS] (arXiv:1405.4683v3, the latest version; J. Math. Soc. Japan 68 (2016)), and uses its notation.
- `R := Z[t_1, …, t_{n+1}]/(t_1^m − 1, …, t_{n+1}^m − 1)`, the group ring of `G ≅ (Z/m)^{n+1}`, which acts on `X` by multiplying the coordinates by `m`-th roots of unity. The variable `t_0 := (t_1⋯t_{n+1})^{−1}` never enters below.
- `φ(u) := u^{m−1} + ⋯ + u + 1`.
- For `J ∈ 𝒥`: `τ_J := Π_{i=0}^{k}(t_{k_i} − 1)` and `ψ_J := τ_J · Π_{i=1}^{k} φ(t_{j_i}t_{k_i})`. Since `j_0 = 0`, the pair containing `0` contributes only the factor `t_{k_0} − 1`.
- `Γ_K ⊆ μ_m^{n+1}` [DS, Definition 1.3] is the set of `(a_1, …, a_{n+1})` with `a_i ≠ 1` for every `i` such that, for some `J ∈ K`, `a_{j_i}a_{k_i} = 1` for `i = 1, …, k`.

We use four results of [DS], collected here.

> **THEOREM 0 [DS].** *Let `n = 2k ≥ 2`, `m ≥ 3`, `∅ ≠ K ⊆ 𝒥`, and `A_K := R/(ψ_J : J ∈ K)`.*
> - *(a) [DS, Theorem 1.1(a)] `Tors(H_n(X)/L_K(X)) ≅ Tors(A_K)`.*
> - *(b) [DS, Claim 4.3] If `K_p` is an algebraically closed field of characteristic `p = 0` or `p ∤ m`, then `dim_{K_p}(A_K ⊗ K_p) = m^{n+1} − |Γ_K|`.*
> - *(c) [DS, Theorem 1.4] `rank L_K(X) = |Γ_K| + 1`.*
> - *(d) [DS, Corollary 1.5] The order of the torsion of `H_n(X)/L_K(X)` is divisible only by primes dividing `m`.*

Part (b) is algebraic: it is a count of points by the Chinese remainder theorem (Appendix B, Step 6). Parts (c) and (d) follow from (a), (b) and the proof of (a) in a few lines. For the reader who wants to check the only topological input, Appendix B gives a self-contained proof of Theorem 0 modulo two results that we do not re-prove: Pham's description of the homology of the affine Fermat variety [Ph], and the intersection numbers of the standard spaces with the Pham cycle [DS, Theorem 2.2].

### 2.1 Torsion as one dimension
**Proposition 2.1.** *Let `∅ ≠ K ⊆ 𝒥`. For every prime `p`, `dim_{F_p}(A_K ⊗ F_p) ≥ m^{n+1} − |Γ_K|`, and `H_n(X)/L_K(X)` is torsion free if and only if equality holds for every prime `p` dividing `m`. More precisely, for each prime `p`, equality holds iff `A_K` has no `p`-torsion, iff `H_n(X)/L_K(X)` has no `p`-torsion.*

*Proof.* `R` is a free `Z`-module of rank `m^{n+1}` (basis `t^ν`, `ν ∈ (Z/m)^{n+1}`), so `A_K` is a finitely generated abelian group; write `A_K ≅ Z^ρ ⊕ ⊕_ℓ T_ℓ` with `T_ℓ` a finite `ℓ`-group. Tensoring with `F_p` gives `dim_{F_p}(A_K ⊗ F_p) = ρ + c_p`, where `c_p` is the number of cyclic factors of `T_p` (since `Z/ℓ^a ⊗ F_p` is `F_p` for `ℓ = p` and `0` otherwise). By Theorem 0(b) with `p = 0`, `ρ = dim_C(A_K ⊗ C) = m^{n+1} − |Γ_K|`. So the inequality holds, and equality at `p` means `T_p = 0`. (This part of the proof uses only Theorem 0(b), which is algebraic.) By Theorem 0(a), `H_n(X)/L_K(X)` is torsion free iff `A_K` is; by Theorem 0(d), only primes dividing `m` can occur. ∎

From now on, until the end of §5, `p` is an odd prime, `v ≥ 1` and `m = q = p^v` (odd `m` that are not prime powers are treated in §6–§7). Then only the prime `p` matters, and `A_K ⊗ F_p = F_p[G]/(ψ̄_J : J ∈ K)`, where `F_p[G] := F_p[t_1, …, t_{2k+1}]/(t_i^q − 1)` and `ψ̄_J` is the image of `ψ_J`.

### 2.2 The count
Let `T` be a finite set of `q − 1` elements with a fixed-point-free involution `u ↦ −u` (for instance `T = F_q^×`). It has `h := (q−1)/2` orbits `{u, −u}`, which we call **classes**.

**Lemma 2.2.** *For odd `q`, `|Γ_𝒥| = Q_k(q)`. More precisely, `|Γ_𝒥|` is the number of `N`-tuples in `μ_q ∖ {1}` whose multiset is closed under inversion, and this is also the number of `N`-tuples in `T` whose multiset is closed under the involution.*

*Proof.* Let `a = (a_1, …, a_{2k+1}) ∈ Γ_J` and put `a_0 := (a_1⋯a_{2k+1})^{−1}`. The pairs `{j_i, k_i}`, `i ≥ 1`, cover `{1, …, 2k+1} ∖ {k_0}` and their products are `1`, so `Π_{i≥1} a_i = a_{k_0}` and `a_0 = a_{k_0}^{−1} ≠ 1`. Hence `a ∈ Γ_𝒥` iff the multiset `{a_0, …, a_{2k+1}} ⊆ μ_q ∖ {1}` splits into inverse pairs. Conversely, if an `N`-tuple in `μ_q ∖ {1}` is closed under inversion, its product is `1`, so it is determined by its last `2k+1` entries. Since `q` is odd, inversion has no fixed point on `μ_q ∖ {1}`, which splits into `h` pairs `{c, c^{−1}}`. By the exponential formula (the generating function of a product of labelled structures is the product of the generating functions), each pair contributes the factor `Σ_b x^{2b}/(b!)^2 = I_0(2x)` (choose `b` coordinates equal to `c` and `b` equal to `c^{−1}`). This gives `Q_k(q)`, and the same computation applies to `T`. ∎

Lemma 2.2 and its proof hold for every odd `m` in place of `q`; §6, §10 and §11 use it in that form. Likewise Lemma 2.3 below holds in any ring `F[t]/((t − 1)^q)` with `F ⊇ F_p` a field and `q = p^v`, and Lemma 2.4(i) over any such field `F`; this is how §6.4 uses them.

*External check.* At `k = 1`, `Q_1(q) = 3(q−1)(q−2)`, so `rank L(X) = 3(m−1)(m−2) + 1`. For `m = 3` this is `7`, the Picard number of the cubic surface, which is generated by its `27` lines. It agrees with the constant-term description of `rank L(X)` in [DS, Remark 4.4].[^1]

[^1]: The polynomials displayed in [DS, Remark 4.4] are, for odd `m`, the corresponding values of `|Γ_𝒥|`, in accordance with the constant-term description `rank L(X) = 1 + |Γ_𝒥|` given there; this is how we quote them in §3.

### 2.3 A coordinate in which the relations become linear
**Lemma 2.3** (identities in characteristic `p`)**.** *In `F_p[t]/(t^q − 1)` put `y := t − t^{−1}`. Then:*
- *(i) `F_p[t]/(t^q − 1) = F_p[y]/(y^q)`, and `t ↦ t^{−1}` becomes `y ↦ −y`;*
- *(ii) in `F_p[t_j, t_k]/(t_j^q − 1, t_k^q − 1)`, `t_jt_k − 1 = (unit)·(y_j + y_k)`;*
- *(iii) `φ(u) = (u − 1)^{q−1}` in `F_p[u]`.*

*Proof.* (i) `t^q − 1 = (t − 1)^q` in characteristic `p`, because `q = p^v`; so `t − 1` is nilpotent, and the ring is local with maximal ideal `(t − 1)`. The elements `t` and `t + 1 = 2 + (t − 1)` are units, the second because `2 ∈ F_p^×`. Hence `y = t^{−1}(t − 1)(t + 1)` is a unit times `t − 1`, so `y^j ≠ 0` for `j ≤ q − 1` and `y^q = (unit)·(t − 1)^q = 0`. The `q` elements `1, y, …, y^{q−1}` lie in successive powers of the maximal ideal and not in the next, so they are linearly independent; the ring has dimension `q`, so it is `F_p[y]/(y^q)`. Inversion sends `t − t^{−1}` to `t^{−1} − t = −y`. (ii) `y_j + y_k = t_j + t_k − t_j^{−1} − t_k^{−1} = (t_j + t_k)(t_jt_k)^{−1}(t_jt_k − 1)`, and `t_j + t_k = 2 + (t_j − 1) + (t_k − 1)` is a unit. (iii) `(u − 1)φ(u) = u^q − 1 = (u − 1)^q`, and `F_p[u]` is a domain. ∎

Write `B := F_p[y_1, …, y_{2k+1}]/(y_1^q, …, y_{2k+1}^q)`; by (i), `F_p[G] ≅ B`. Put

`D(a, b) := Σ_{u=0}^{q−2} (−1)^u a^u b^{q−2−u} = (b^{q−1} − a^{q−1})/(a + b)`.

The second expression holds in `Z[a,b]` because `q − 2` is odd. `D` is antisymmetric, `D(b, a) = −D(a, b)`, and at `q = 3` it is `D(a, b) = b − a`.

**Lemma 2.4** (identities in characteristic `p`)**.** *(i) `(a + b)^{q−1}·b = −ab·D(a, b)` in `F_p[a,b]/(b^q)`. (ii) For every `J ∈ 𝒥`, `ψ̄_J = (unit)·Y·D_J` in `B`, where `Y := y_1 y_2 ⋯ y_{2k+1}` and `D_J := Π_{i=1}^{k} D(y_{j_i}, y_{k_i})` is the product over the `k` pairs of `J` that do not contain `0`.*

*Proof.* (i) The sum `(a + b)·Σ_{s=0}^{q−1}(−1)^s a^s b^{q−1−s}` telescopes to `(−1)^{q−1}a^q + b^q = a^q + b^q`, which is `(a + b)^q` in characteristic `p`. So `(a + b)^{q−1} = Σ_{s=0}^{q−1}(−1)^s a^s b^{q−1−s}` in the domain `F_p[a,b]`. Multiply by `b` and drop `b^q`: `Σ_{s=1}^{q−1}(−1)^s a^s b^{q−s} = −ab·Σ_{u=0}^{q−2}(−1)^u a^u b^{q−2−u}`. (ii) By Lemma 2.3, up to a unit, `ψ̄_J = Π_{i=0}^{k} y_{k_i} · Π_{i=1}^{k}(y_{j_i} + y_{k_i})^{q−1}`. By (i), `y_{k_i}(y_{j_i} + y_{k_i})^{q−1} = −y_{j_i}y_{k_i}D(y_{j_i}, y_{k_i})` for each `i ≥ 1`. The indices `{k_0} ∪ ⋃_{i≥1}{j_i, k_i}` are exactly `{1, …, 2k+1}`, so the monomial factors multiply to `Y`. ∎

### 2.4 The sum form (S)
Let `C := F_p[y_1, …, y_{2k+1}]/(y_1^{q−1}, …, y_{2k+1}^{q−1})`, of dimension `(q−1)^{2k+1}`.

**Proposition 2.5 (the translation).** *Let `q = p^v`, `k ≥ 1` and `∅ ≠ K ⊆ 𝒥`. Then `dim_{F_p} F_p[G]/(ψ̄_J : J ∈ K) = q^{2k+1} − dim_{F_p}(D_J : J ∈ K)C`, and*
- *`dim_{F_p}(D_J : J ∈ K)C ≤ |Γ_K|` always;*
- *`H_{2k}(X)/L_K(X)` is torsion free iff `dim_{F_p}(D_J : J ∈ K)C = |Γ_K|`.*

*In particular (`K = 𝒥`), the Main Theorem at `(k, q)` is equivalent to*

**(S)**   `dim_{F_p} (D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)·C = Q_k(q)`.

*Proof.* Multiplication by `Y` on `B` kills exactly the monomials having some exponent `≥ q − 1`, so its kernel is `(y_1^{q−1}, …, y_{2k+1}^{q−1})B`, and it induces an injective linear map `C → B`, `f ↦ Yf`. By Lemma 2.4(ii), the ideal `(ψ̄_J : J ∈ K)B = (Y D_J : J ∈ K)B` is the image of the ideal `(D_J : J ∈ K)C` under this map, so the two have the same dimension. The two claims follow from Proposition 2.1 and Lemma 2.2. ∎

*Remarks 2.5′.* (1) The generators `D_J` all have degree `k(q−2)`. (2) **What the topology is needed for.** The inequality `dim(D_J)C ≤ Q_k(q)` can also be proved with no topology: it is part of Theorem 5.9, and it also follows from Propositions 2.6 and 9.1. The inequality is also the case `p | m` of Proposition 2.1, whose proof uses only Theorem 0(b), a Chinese-remainder count. [DS] is needed only for two things: that equality in (S) is *equivalent* to torsion-freeness, and the rank of `L(X)`. (3) [DS, §5] formulate a computational criterion of the same kind in the ring `R̄ := Z[t_1, …, t_{n+1}]/(φ(t_1), …, φ(t_{n+1}))`, through their Lemma 4.5 (`ψ_J = ±λρ_J`, `λ = Π(t_i − 1)`). We do not use it.

### 2.5 An equivalent intersection form
**Proposition 2.6.** *Let `I'_J := (y_{j_i} + y_{k_i} : i = 1, …, k)` and `b_k(r) := dim F_p[y_1, …, y_{2k+1}] / ⋂_{J∈𝒥}(I'_J + (y_1^r, …, y_{2k+1}^r))`. Then `dim(D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)C = b_k(q − 1)`, so (S) is equivalent to `b_k(q − 1) = Q_k(q)`. Eliminating `x_0 := −(y_1 + ⋯ + y_{2k+1})`, `b_k(r) = dim F_p[x_0, …, x_{2k+1}] / ⋂_{J∈𝒥}(I_J + (x_0^r, …, x_{2k+1}^r))` with `I_J := (x_a + x_b : {a,b} ∈ J)`.*

*Proof.* On `B` put `⟨f, g⟩ :=` the coefficient of `Π_i y_i^{q−1}` in `fg`. Then `⟨y^α, y^β⟩ = 1` if `α + β = (q−1, …, q−1)` and `0` otherwise, a permutation matrix, so the pairing is non-degenerate. For an ideal `I ⊆ B`, `⟨f, gh⟩ = ⟨fg, h⟩` shows that `f ∈ ann_B(I)` iff `f ⊥ I`; hence `dim ann_B(I) = dim B − dim I`, and `ann_B(Σ_J I_J) = ⋂_J ann_B(I_J)`. So `dim(ψ̄_J : J)B = dim B/⋂_J ann_B(ψ̄_J)`, which equals `dim(D_J : J)C` by the proof of Proposition 2.5. For a fixed `J`, the linear forms `w := y_{k_0}`, `u_i := y_{j_i} + y_{k_i}`, `v_i := y_{k_i}` (`i ≥ 1`) form a basis of `F_p y_1 ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ F_p y_{2k+1}`. Since `ℓ^q = Σ c_i y_i^q` for every linear form `ℓ = Σ c_i y_i` over `F_p` (Frobenius, and `c^q = c` on `F_p`), the ideal `(y_i^q)` is also generated by `w^q, u_i^q, v_i^q`. In these coordinates `ψ̄_J = (unit)·w·Π_{i≥1} v_i u_i^{q−1}`, a monomial times a unit, whose annihilator in the box ring is the monomial ideal `(w^{q−1}) + (v_i^{q−1}, u_i : i ≥ 1)`. Modulo `u_i` one has `y_{j_i} ≡ −v_i`, so this ideal is `I'_J + (y_1^{q−1}, …, y_{2k+1}^{q−1})`. Finally `y_i^q ∈ (y_i^{q−1})`, so `B/⋂_J(…) = F_p[y]/⋂_J(I'_J + (y_i^{q−1}))`. For the last sentence, put `σ := x_0 + ⋯ + x_{2k+1}` and substitute `y_i = x_i` (`i ≥ 1`), `x_0 = −Σ y_i`. Two facts are used. (1) Every `I_J` contains `σ` (it is the sum of the generators `x_a + x_b`); by the correspondence between the ideals of `S` containing `σ` and the ideals of `S/(σ) ≅ F_p[y]`, the intersection of the ideals `I_J + (x_i^r)` maps onto the intersection of their images. (2) Modulo `I_J`, `x_0 ≡ −x_{k_0}`, so `x_0^r ∈ I_J + (x_1^r, …, x_{2k+1}^r)`, and the image of `I_J + (x_0^r, …, x_{2k+1}^r)` is `I'_J + (y_1^r, …, y_{2k+1}^r)`. ∎

By Matlis duality this form is **equivalent** to (S), not weaker: it is a restatement, not a reduction. It is used in §9.

### 2.6 A worked example: the cubic surface, `(k, q) = (1, 3)`
Here `n = 2`, `N = 4`, and `𝒥` has three matchings, `[[0,1],[2,3]]`, `[[0,2],[1,3]]`, `[[0,3],[1,2]]`. The pairs avoiding `0` are `(2,3)`, `(1,3)`, `(1,2)`, and at `q = 3`, `D(a,b) = b − a`, so the generators are `y_3 − y_2`, `y_3 − y_1`, `y_2 − y_1` in `C = F_3[y_1, y_2, y_3]/(y_1^2, y_2^2, y_3^2)`, of dimension `8`. The ideal they generate has dimension `2` in degree `1` (two of the three are independent), `3` in degree `2` (`y_1(y_2 − y_1) = y_1y_2`, `y_1(y_3 − y_1) = y_1y_3`, and `y_2(y_3 − y_1) = y_2y_3 − y_1y_2`), and `1` in degree `3`: in total `6`. On the other side, `T = F_3^× = {1, 2}`, and `Γ'` (§3) consists of the tuples in `T^3` with one negation pair, i.e. the `2^3 − 2 = 6` non-constant tuples. So (S) holds, `rank L(X) = 7`, and `dim F_3[G]/(ψ̄_J) = 3^3 − 6 = 21`.

## 3. The count

By Lemma 2.2, `Q_k(q) = N!·[x^N] I_0(2x)^h` with `N = 2k+2` counts the `N`-tuples in `T` whose multiset is closed under the involution. In small cases:

**The zero-free point set.** For the proof we use a set of points of the same cardinality. For a tuple `M` in `T` let `ν(M)` be the largest number of pairwise disjoint pairs of indices `{a, b}` with `y_b = −y_a`, and put

`Γ' := {y ∈ T^{2k+1} : ν(y) = k}`.

**Lemma 3.1.** *`|Γ'| = Q_k(q)`.*

*Proof.* In each class `{u, −u}`, a tuple `M` has `a` entries equal to `u` and `ā` equal to `−u`; the class contributes `min(a, ā)` disjoint negation pairs, and `ν(M)` is the sum of these contributions, since negation pairs never leave a class. If `ν(y) = k` for `y ∈ T^{2k+1}`, then exactly one entry is left unpaired, with a value `w`. Appending `−w` gives an `N`-tuple closed under the involution. Conversely, if an `N`-tuple is closed under the involution, deleting its last entry `z` leaves a `(2k+1)`-tuple with `ν = k` whose unpaired value is `−z`, so the last entry is determined by the others. This is a bijection onto the closed `N`-tuples, which number `Q_k(q)` by Lemma 2.2. ∎

## 4. Warm-up: the cubic case q = 3

For `q = 3` the proof of §5 applies, but there is a shorter proof that also produces an explicit basis. It works over every field; we write it over `F_3`. Put `n' := 2k+1`. Then `C = F_3[y_1, …, y_{n'}]/(y_i^2)` has the basis `y_U := Π_{i∈U} y_i`, `U ⊆ [n'] := {1, …, n'}`, and `D(a, b) = b − a`, so `D_J = Π_{(a,b)} (y_b − y_a)` over the `k` pairs `(a < b)` of `J` not containing `0`. Order the monomials lexicographically with `y_1 > y_2 > ⋯ > y_{n'}`.

For `U ⊆ [n']`, read `i = 1, …, n'` as a path: an up-step if `i ∈ U`, a down-step if `i ∉ U`. Let `h(i) := |U ∩ [i]| − |[i] ∖ U|`, and let `𝓤 := {U : h(i) ≥ −1 for all i}` (paths that never go below `−1`).

**Theorem 4.1.** *For every `k ≥ 0`, `dim_{F_3}(D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)C = C(2k+2, k+1) = Q_k(3)`. The lexicographic leading monomials of the ideal are exactly the `y_U`, `U ∈ 𝓤`, and its graded ranks are `C(n', u) − C(n', u+2)` in degree `u = k, …, n'`. Hence Conjecture 1.2 of [DS] holds for the cubic Fermat variety of every even dimension.*

*Proof.* *(i) Leading monomials of certificate elements.* For `T ⊆ [n']` and `J ∈ 𝒥` with `{0, c} ∈ J`, the element `y_T·D_J` is `y_c^{[c ∈ T]}·Π_{(a,b)} f_{a,b}`, where `f_{a,b} = y_b − y_a` if `{a,b} ∩ T = ∅` («untouched»), `f_{a,b} = y_ay_b` if `a ∈ T ∌ b`, `f_{a,b} = −y_ay_b` if `b ∈ T ∌ a`, and `f_{a,b} = 0` if `{a,b} ⊆ T`. The factors involve disjoint sets of variables, so a non-zero `y_T D_J` is a sum of `2^{#untouched}` distinct squarefree monomials with coefficients `±1`. The lexicographically largest one chooses `−y_a` in every untouched pair: two of these monomials first differ at the smaller element of a pair where the choices differ, and that element is an `a`. So `LM(y_T D_J) = y_U` with coefficient `±1`, where `U = {a : (a,b) untouched} ∪ ⋃_{touched}{a, b} ∪ ({c} ∩ T)`.

*(ii) Every `U ∈ 𝓤` is reached.* Match the steps greedily: each down-step `i ∉ U` is matched with the nearest earlier unmatched up-step. A down-step stays unmatched exactly when the path reaches a new minimum below `0` at it, so the number of unmatched down-steps is `max(0, −min_i h(i)) ≤ 1`.
- If there is one unmatched down-step, call it `c`. The number of unmatched up-steps is then `|U| − (n' − |U| − 1)`, which is even.
- If there is none, `2|U| − n'` up-steps are unmatched. This number is odd and `≥ −1`, hence `≥ 1`; let `c` be the largest unmatched up-step. An even number remain.

Pair the remaining unmatched up-steps consecutively into «full pairs». The matched pairs `(a < b)`, the full pairs and `{0, c}` form a matching `J ∈ 𝒥`. Let `T` consist of the smaller element of each full pair, together with `c` if `c ∈ U`. Then every matched pair is untouched and every full pair is touched exactly once, so by (i) `LM(y_T D_J) = ±y_U`. Conversely, if `y_U` is a leading monomial as in (i), then every element of `[n'] ∖ U` other than possibly `c` is the larger element `b` of an untouched pair whose smaller element `a` lies in `U`. This injection shows that at most one down-step is unmatched, so `U ∈ 𝓤`.

*(iii) Counting.* Paths of length `n'` with `u` up-steps number `C(n', u)`. By reflecting the part after the first visit to `−2` (André's reflection), those that visit `−2` are in bijection with the paths with `n' − u − 2` up-steps, of which there are `C(n', u+2)`. For `u ≤ k − 1` the endpoint `2u − n' ≤ −3` forces a visit to `−2`. So `#{U ∈ 𝓤 : |U| = u} = C(n', u) − C(n', u+2)` for `u ≥ k` and `0` otherwise. The sum telescopes to `C(n', k) + C(n', k+1) = C(2k+2, k+1)`.

*(iv) Conclusion.* Elements of a subspace with pairwise distinct leading monomials are linearly independent, in any characteristic: the leading monomial of a non-trivial combination is the largest leading monomial that occurs with a non-zero coefficient. Hence `dim(D_J)C ≥ |𝓤| = C(2k+2, k+1) = Q_k(3)`. Equality holds by Theorem 5.9 (or by Proposition 2.5), and it forces the set of leading monomials of the ideal to be exactly `{y_U : U ∈ 𝓤}`, which gives the graded ranks. ∎

*Remarks 4.2.* (1) The graded ranks `C(n', k+j) − C(n', k+j+2)` are the ballot numbers `C(2k+2, k+1−j)(2j+1)/(k+2+j)` (OEIS A039599). For `k = 1, …, 6` they are `2,3,1` · `5,9,5,1` · `14,28,20,7,1` · `42,90,75,35,9,1` · `132,297,275,154,54,11,1` · `429,1001,1001,637,273,77,13,1`. (2) The proof uses the full family: step (ii) needs matchings that are arbitrary. For a subfamily the same argument only bounds the union of the leading-monomial sets, and at `q = 3`, `k = 2` there are subfamilies for which the analogue of (S) fails (§9). (3) *An alternative route, not needed here.* In the `N`-variable form of Proposition 2.6, the span of the generators at `q = 3` is spanned by the Specht polynomials of the two-row shape `(k+1, k)` of `S_{2k+1}`, and the degree-`(N−r)` part of the ideal can be identified with the Specht module `S^{(N−r, r)}`, whose dimension does not depend on the field [Jam]. We checked this identification at `N = 4, 6, 8` (dimensions `6, 20, 70`), but we have not written it out as a proof, and Theorem 4.1 does not depend on it.

## 5. The proof of Theorem B

Throughout this section, `F` is an arbitrary field, `q ≥ 3` is odd, `h = (q−1)/2`, and `T` is a set of `q − 1` elements with a fixed-point-free involution `u ↦ −u` (so `T` has `h` classes `{u, −u}`). For `m ≥ 0` put

`C_m := F[y_1, …, y_m]/(y_1^{q−1}, …, y_m^{q−1})`,   so `C_F = C_{2k+1}`.

The field and the set `T` never interact: `F` carries the ideals, `T` carries the point sets, and the two are compared only through dimensions and cardinalities. Every identity used is an identity of polynomials with integer coefficients. The field reappears in §5.9, where we evaluate the generators on a copy of `T` inside `F`.

**The idea in one paragraph.** Peeling off `y_1` splits an ideal `V` into `q − 1` nested slices, and a point set `Z` into `q − 1` nested layers (Lemma 5.1). If each layer is the point set of an ideal contained in the corresponding slice, induction on `m` gives `dim V ≥ |Z|`. To make this possible we index both sides by down-sets of partitions: `V_Λ` is generated by products of divided differences and Vandermonde determinants, and `Z_Λ` is the set of tuples whose unpaired part has shape in `Λ`. The number of values of `y_1` that complete a tail depends only on the shape `μ` of the tail (P1, §5.4). The `q − 1` shapes obtained from `μ` form a chain for weak dominance (§5.5), so the layers are again down-sets (P2, §5.6). And each generator of a layer lifts to the right slice by one of three explicit constructions (P3, §5.7).

### 5.1 Peeling one variable
Write `C_m = C_{m−1}[y_1]/(y_1^{q−1})`, where `C_{m−1}` is the ring in `y_2, …, y_m`. For an ideal `V ⊆ C_m` and `0 ≤ j ≤ q−2` put

`V_{≤j} := {f ∈ V : deg_{y_1} f ≤ j}`,   `W_j(V) := {[y_1^j] f : f ∈ V_{≤j}} ⊆ C_{m−1}`.

**Lemma 5.1 (peeling; linear algebra and counting).** *(i) `W_j(V)` is an ideal of `C_{m−1}`. (ii) `W_j(V) ⊆ W_{j+1}(V)` for `0 ≤ j < q−2`. (iii) `dim V = Σ_{j=0}^{q−2} dim W_j(V)`. (iv) For `Z ⊆ T^m`, let `F(M') := {y ∈ T : (y, M') ∈ Z}` for `M' ∈ T^{m−1}` and `Z_{>i} := {M' : |F(M')| > i}`. Then `|Z| = Σ_{i=0}^{q−2} |Z_{>i}|`.*

*Proof.* (i) For `g ∈ C_{m−1}` and `f ∈ V_{≤j}`, `gf ∈ V_{≤j}` and `[y_1^j](gf) = g·[y_1^j]f`. (ii) If `f ∈ V_{≤j}`, then `y_1 f ∈ V_{≤j+1}` and `[y_1^{j+1}](y_1 f) = [y_1^j] f`; this uses `y_1^{j+1} ≠ 0`, i.e. `j+1 ≤ q−2`. (iii) The map `V_{≤j} → W_j(V)`, `f ↦ [y_1^j]f`, is onto with kernel `V_{≤j−1}`, and `V = V_{≤q−2}`. (iv) `|Z| = Σ_{M'} |F(M')|`. Since `|F(M')| ≤ q − 1`, each `M'` lies in `Z_{>i}` for exactly the `|F(M')|` values `i = 0, …, |F(M')| − 1`, so `Σ_i |Z_{>i}| = Σ_{M'} |F(M')|`. ∎

### 5.2 Partitions, residues and weak dominance
- **Residue.** Regard `M ∈ T^m` as a multiset of values. In each class `{u, −u}` let `a`, `ā` be the multiplicities of `u` and `−u` in `M`. The class contributes `min(a, ā)` negation pairs, and `ν(M) = Σ_{classes} min(a, ā)`, since a negation pair lies inside one class and the classes are disjoint. The **residue** keeps, in each class with `a ≠ ā`, `|a − ā|` copies of the majority value.
- **`λ(M)`** is the partition formed by the multiplicities of the residue. Distinct residual values lie in distinct classes, so `ℓ(λ(M)) ≤ h`, and `|λ(M)| = Σ|a − ā| = m − 2ν(M) ≡ m (mod 2)`.
- **`Par_m^{(h)}`** `:= {λ : |λ| ≤ m, |λ| ≡ m (mod 2), ℓ(λ) ≤ h}`. The cap `ℓ(λ) ≤ h` matters: for example `(1^m) ∉ Par_m^{(h)}` when `m > h`. We write `Par_m` when `h` is fixed.
- **Weak dominance.** For a partition `λ` let `S_t(λ)` be the sum of its `t` largest parts, padded with zeros. Put `λ ≼ μ` iff `S_t(λ) ≤ S_t(μ)` for all `t ≥ 1`. The order is defined also between partitions of different sizes; it is a partial order, not a total one (`(2)` and `(1,1,1)` are incomparable).
- For a `≼`-down-set `Λ ⊆ Par_m`, `Z_Λ := {M ∈ T^m : λ(M) ∈ Λ}`.
- **Tight patterns.** For `λ ∈ Par_m` and an index set `I` with `|I| = m`, a tight pattern of `λ` on `I` consists of `p = (m − |λ|)/2` pairwise disjoint pairs `P` of elements of `I`, together with pairwise disjoint blocks `B_1, …, B_{λ_1}` with `|B_c| = λ'_c` (the column lengths of `λ`), which partition the rest of `I`. Its **product** is `D_P · Π_c Δ(B_c)`, where `D_P := Π_{(a<b)∈P} D(y_a, y_b)` and, for `B = {b_1 < ⋯ < b_r}`, `Δ(B) := det(y_{b_c}^{e−1})_{e,c=1..r} = Π_{c<c'}(y_{b_{c'}} − y_{b_c})` (the Vandermonde determinant, with `Δ(∅) = Δ({b}) = 1`).
- The blocks are indexed by the *columns* of `λ`. In the proof of Theorem 5.9 a row of `λ` carries one value and a column carries pairwise distinct values.
- `V_Λ ⊆ C_m` is the ideal generated by the products of all tight patterns on `[m]` of all `λ ∈ Λ`. For `m = 0` the empty product is `1`.

**Lemma 5.2 (the root; the only place where the full family enters).** *In `Par_{2k+1}`, `Λ = {(1)}` is a down-set, `V_{(1)} = (D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)C_{2k+1}`, and `Z_{(1)} = Γ'`, so `|Z_{(1)}| = Q_k(q)`.*

*Proof.* If `λ ≼ (1)` then `S_t(λ) ≤ 1` for all `t`, so `|λ| ≤ 1`; since `|λ|` is odd, `λ = (1)`. A tight pattern of `(1)` on `[2k+1]` consists of `k` disjoint pairs and one singleton block (with `Δ = 1`), so its product is `D_P` for a set `P` of `k` disjoint pairs covering `[2k+1] ∖ {c}`. Adding the pair `{0, c}` gives a matching `J ∈ 𝒥`, and every `J` arises in this way; since `D` is antisymmetric, `D_P = ±D_J`. So `V_{(1)}` contains `D_J` for *every* matching `J`: this is where the whole family `𝒥` is used, and a subfamily would give a smaller ideal. Finally `λ(M) = (1)` iff `ν(M) = k`, so `Z_{(1)} = Γ'`, and `|Γ'| = Q_k(q)` by Lemma 3.1. ∎

### 5.3 The theorem
**Theorem 5.3.** *For every field `F`, every odd `q ≥ 3`, every `m ≥ 0` and every `≼`-down-set `Λ ⊆ Par_m^{(h)}`, `dim_F V_Λ ≥ |Z_Λ|`.*

**Corollary 5.4.** *Theorem B holds, and so does (S) for every `k ≥ 0` and every `q = p^v`, `p` an odd prime. Hence the Main Theorem.*

*Proof of the Corollary.* By Lemma 5.2 and Theorem 5.3 with `m = 2k+1` and `Λ = {(1)}`, `dim_F(D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)C_F ≥ |Γ'| = Q_k(q)`. The equality clause and the integral statement of Theorem B are Theorems 5.9 and 5.9′ below (with Lemma 5.2, which holds verbatim over `Z`); for (S) the reverse inequality is also Proposition 2.5. Proposition 2.5 then turns (S) into the Main Theorem. ∎

The proof of Theorem 5.3 is by induction on `m`, with `q` fixed, and occupies §5.4–§5.8. We fix `m ≥ 1`, a down-set `Λ ⊆ Par_m`, and peel `y_1` (Lemma 5.1).

### 5.4 (P1) The fibres depend only on the residue partition
Take `Z = Z_Λ` in Lemma 5.1(iv). Fix `M' ∈ T^{m−1}` (indices `2, …, m`) with `λ(M') = μ`, residual values `u_1, …, u_ℓ` in distinct classes, and multiplicities `μ_1 ≥ ⋯ ≥ μ_ℓ`. Adding a value `y` for the index `1` gives:
- `y = −u_j`: the class of `u_j` gains a negation pair, and `λ(y, M') = μ − e_j` (one box removed from row `j`, re-sorted);
- `y = u_j`: `λ(y, M') = μ + e_j`;
- `y` in one of the `h − ℓ` classes absent from the residue (where `M'` has equal multiplicities, possibly `0 = 0`): `λ(y, M') = μ ⊔ 1` (a new part equal to `1`). There are `2(h − ℓ) = q − 1 − 2ℓ` such `y`.

These are `ℓ + ℓ + (q−1−2ℓ) = q − 1 = |T|` values. Hence `|F(M')| = F_Λ(μ)`, where

`F_Λ(μ) := #{j : μ − e_j ∈ Λ} + #{j : μ + e_j ∈ Λ} + (q − 1 − 2ℓ)·[μ ⊔ 1 ∈ Λ]`,

which depends only on `μ`. So `Z_{>i} = Z_{Λ_i}` with `Λ_i := {μ ∈ Par_{m−1} : F_Λ(μ) > i}`. All the options lie in `Par_m`: their size is `|μ| ± 1 ≤ m`, with the parity of `m`, and `μ ⊔ 1` only occurs when `ℓ < h`.

### 5.5 (Chain) The options form a chain
Let `r̄_j` be the last row of `μ` of length `μ_j`, and `r̲_j` the first. Removing a box from row `j` lowers the last row of that length, and adding a box to row `j` raises the first one, so for all `t ≥ 1`:

`S_t(μ − e_j) = S_t(μ) − [t ≥ r̄_j]`,   `S_t(μ + e_j) = S_t(μ) + [t ≥ r̲_j]`,   `S_t(μ ⊔ 1) = S_t(μ) + [t > ℓ]`.

List the `L := q − 1` options by position `p = 1, …, L`:

`μ − e_1 ≼ μ − e_2 ≼ ⋯ ≼ μ − e_ℓ ≼ μ ⊔ 1 = ⋯ = μ ⊔ 1 ≼ μ + e_ℓ ≼ ⋯ ≼ μ + e_1`

with `μ − e_p` at position `p ≤ ℓ`, the `q − 1 − 2ℓ` copies of `μ ⊔ 1` at positions `ℓ < p ≤ L − ℓ`, and `μ + e_j` at position `L + 1 − j`.

**Lemma 5.5 (inequality of partitions).** *The options at positions `1, …, L` are weakly increasing for `≼`. Consequently the options lying in `Λ` form an initial segment, and `F_Λ(μ)` is the length of that segment.*

*Proof.* `r̄_j` is non-decreasing in `j` (rows are sorted), so `μ − e_j ≼ μ − e_{j+1}`. Next, `μ − e_ℓ ≼ μ ≼ μ ⊔ 1`. Then `μ ⊔ 1 ≼ μ + e_ℓ`, because `r̲_ℓ ≤ ℓ < t` whenever `[t > ℓ] = 1`. Finally `r̲_j` is non-decreasing in `j`, so `μ + e_{j+1} ≼ μ + e_j`. Since `Λ` is a down-set, `opt_p ∈ Λ` implies `opt_{p'} ∈ Λ` for all `p' ≤ p`. ∎

In particular the removals in `Λ` are `μ − e_1, …, μ − e_r` for some `r ≤ ℓ`. Since rows of equal length give equal partitions, the options at the positions of equal rows coincide, so `r = 0` or `r` is the last row of its length. The additions in `Λ` are `μ + e_j` for `j_0 ≤ j ≤ ℓ`, with `j_0` (if it exists) the first row of its length. Put `ι := [μ ⊔ 1 ∈ Λ]` (so `ι = 0` when `ℓ = h`). If some addition lies in `Λ`, then `r = ℓ`, and `ι = 1` whenever `ℓ < h`; if `ι = 1` then `r = ℓ`. Therefore

`F_Λ(μ) = r + (q − 1 − 2ℓ)·ι + (ℓ − j_0 + 1)·[j_0 exists]`.   (5.1)

### 5.6 (P2) The layers are down-sets
**Proposition 5.6 (inequality of partitions).** *If `μ, μ̃ ∈ Par_{m−1}` and `μ ≼ μ̃`, then `opt_p(μ) ≼ opt_p(μ̃)` for every position `p`. Hence `F_Λ(μ) ≥ F_Λ(μ̃)`, and every `Λ_i` is a `≼`-down-set of `Par_{m−1}`.*

The second sentence follows from the first: if `opt_p(μ̃) ∈ Λ` then `opt_p(μ) ∈ Λ`, so `F_Λ(μ̃) ≤ F_Λ(μ)`, and `μ̃ ∈ Λ_i` implies `μ ∈ Λ_i`. We prove the first sentence. Write `ℓ = ℓ(μ)`, `ℓ̃ = ℓ(μ̃)`, and mark with a tilde the quantities of `μ̃`. Recall that `|μ| ≡ |μ̃| (mod 2)`, since both lie in `Par_{m−1}`. Two facts are used throughout.
- **(T)** If `t ≥ 1` is **tight**, i.e. `S_t(μ) = S_t(μ̃)`, then `μ_t ≥ μ̃_t` (from `S_{t−1}(μ) ≤ S_{t−1}(μ̃)`) and `μ_{t+1} ≤ μ̃_{t+1}` (from `S_{t+1}(μ) ≤ S_{t+1}(μ̃)`).
- **(G)** Each comparison below has the form `S_t(μ) + δ ≤ S_t(μ̃) + δ̃` with `δ, δ̃ ∈ {−1, 0, 1}`. If `δ − δ̃ ≤ 1`, it can only fail at a tight `t` with `δ = δ̃ + 1`. The one place where `δ − δ̃ = 2` is possible is treated explicitly, in (d1).

The positions fall into seven cases. A removal of one partition is never compared with an addition of the other, because `ℓ + ℓ̃ ≤ 2h = q − 1` leaves the middle positions of `μ` in between.

| case | positions | option of `μ` | option of `μ̃` | only possible failure | why it cannot occur |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) | `p ≤ min(ℓ, ℓ̃)` | `μ − e_p` | `μ̃ − e_p` | tight `t`, `r̄̃_p ≤ t < r̄_p` | forces `S_{p−1}(μ̃) < S_{p−1}(μ)` |
| (b) | `j ≤ min(ℓ, ℓ̃)` | `μ + e_j` | `μ̃ + e_j` | tight `t`, `r̲_j ≤ t < r̲̃_j` | forces `S_j(μ̃) < S_j(μ)` |
| (c) | middle | `μ ⊔ 1` | `μ̃ ⊔ 1` | `ℓ < t ≤ ℓ̃` | the rows `ℓ+1, …, t` of `μ̃`, (5.2) |
| (d1) | `ℓ < p ≤ ℓ̃` | `μ ⊔ 1` | `μ̃ − e_p` | `δ − δ̃ = 2` | (5.2) and parity |
| (d2) | `ℓ < j ≤ ℓ̃` | `μ ⊔ 1` | `μ̃ + e_j` | none | (5.2) |
| (e1) | `ℓ̃ < p ≤ ℓ` | `μ − e_p` | `μ̃ ⊔ 1` | none | chain `μ − e_p ≼ μ ≼ μ̃ ≼ μ̃ ⊔ 1` |
| (e2) | `ℓ̃ < j ≤ ℓ` | `μ + e_j` | `μ̃ ⊔ 1` | tight `t ≤ ℓ̃` | forces `S_j(μ̃) < S_j(μ)` |

*(a) Removal against removal, `p ≤ min(ℓ, ℓ̃)`.* We need `S_t(μ) − [t ≥ r̄_p] ≤ S_t(μ̃) − [t ≥ r̄̃_p]`. The only danger is a tight `t` with `r̄̃_p ≤ t < r̄_p`. Then `t ≥ p`, and rows `p, …, t+1` of `μ` all have length `μ_p`; in particular `μ_t = μ_{t+1} = μ_p`. Since `t + 1 > r̄̃_p`, `μ̃_{t+1} < μ̃_p`. By (T), `μ̃_{t+1} ≥ μ_{t+1} = μ_t ≥ μ̃_t ≥ μ̃_{t+1}`, so `μ̃_t = μ̃_{t+1} = μ_p` and `μ̃_p > μ_p`. Hence `S_t(μ̃) − S_{p−1}(μ̃) = Σ_{s=p}^{t} μ̃_s ≥ μ̃_p + (t − p)μ_p > (t − p + 1)μ_p = S_t(μ) − S_{p−1}(μ)`. Since `t` is tight, `S_{p−1}(μ̃) < S_{p−1}(μ)`, contradicting `μ ≼ μ̃`.

*(b) Addition against addition, `j ≤ min(ℓ, ℓ̃)` (position `L + 1 − j`).* We need `S_t(μ) + [t ≥ r̲_j] ≤ S_t(μ̃) + [t ≥ r̲̃_j]`. The only danger is a tight `t` with `r̲_j ≤ t < r̲̃_j`. Then `t < r̲̃_j ≤ j`, so `μ_t = μ_{t+1} = μ_j`, and `μ̃_t > μ̃_j` because `t` lies before the first row of `μ̃` of length `μ̃_j`. By (T), `μ̃_{t+1} ≥ μ_{t+1} = μ_j = μ_t ≥ μ̃_t ≥ μ̃_{t+1}`, so `μ̃_t = μ̃_{t+1} = μ_j > μ̃_j`. Every `μ̃_s` with `s > t` is at most `μ_j`, and `μ̃_j < μ_j`, so `Σ_{s=t+1}^{j} μ̃_s < (j − t)μ_j = Σ_{s=t+1}^{j} μ_s`. With tightness at `t`, `S_j(μ̃) < S_j(μ)`: a contradiction.

*(c) Middle against middle.* We need `S_t(μ) + [t > ℓ] ≤ S_t(μ̃) + [t > ℓ̃]`. If `ℓ ≥ ℓ̃` this is immediate. If `ℓ < ℓ̃` and `ℓ < t ≤ ℓ̃`, the rows `ℓ+1, …, t` of `μ̃` are non-empty, so

`S_t(μ̃) ≥ S_ℓ(μ̃) + (t − ℓ) ≥ S_ℓ(μ) + (t − ℓ) = |μ| + (t − ℓ) ≥ |μ| + 1 = S_t(μ) + 1`.   (5.2)

*Layout of the positions.* If `ℓ = ℓ̃` only (a), (b), (c) occur. If `ℓ < ℓ̃`, positions `ℓ+1, …, ℓ̃` compare a middle option of `μ` with a removal of `μ̃` (case d1), and positions `L+1−j` with `ℓ < j ≤ ℓ̃` compare a middle option of `μ` with an addition of `μ̃` (case d2). These positions are middle positions of `μ` because `ℓ + ℓ̃ ≤ 2h = L`. If `ℓ > ℓ̃`, the roles are reversed (cases e1, e2).

*(d1) `ℓ < p ≤ ℓ̃`: `μ ⊔ 1` against `μ̃ − e_p`.* We need `S_t(μ) + [t > ℓ] ≤ S_t(μ̃) − [t ≥ r̄̃_p]`. Here `δ − δ̃` can be `2`, so we check every `t`.
- `t ≤ ℓ`: both brackets vanish, since `r̄̃_p ≥ p > ℓ ≥ t`.
- `t ≥ ℓ̃`: `S_t(μ̃) = |μ̃| ≥ |μ| + (ℓ̃ − ℓ) ≥ |μ| + 1` by (5.2) at `t = ℓ̃`; by parity `|μ̃| ≥ |μ| + 2`, which suffices.
- `ℓ + 2 ≤ t < ℓ̃`: `S_t(μ̃) ≥ |μ| + (t − ℓ) ≥ |μ| + 2` by (5.2).
- `t = ℓ + 1 < ℓ̃`: if `t < r̄̃_p`, (5.2) suffices. If `t ≥ r̄̃_p`, then `r̄̃_p = p = ℓ + 1` and we need `S_{ℓ+1}(μ̃) ≥ |μ| + 2`. If `μ̃_{ℓ+1} ≥ 2` this holds, since `S_ℓ(μ̃) ≥ |μ|`. If `μ̃_{ℓ+1} = 1`, then row `ℓ+2 ≤ ℓ̃` of `μ̃` also has length `1`, so `r̄̃_{ℓ+1} ≥ ℓ + 2 > t`, a contradiction.

*(d2) `ℓ < j ≤ ℓ̃`: `μ ⊔ 1` against `μ̃ + e_j`.* We need `S_t(μ) + [t > ℓ] ≤ S_t(μ̃) + [t ≥ r̲̃_j]`. For `t ≤ ℓ` this is immediate. For `ℓ < t ≤ ℓ̃` it follows from (5.2), and for `t > ℓ̃` from `|μ̃| ≥ |μ| + 1`.

*(e1) `ℓ̃ < p ≤ ℓ`: `μ − e_p` against `μ̃ ⊔ 1`* (which exists because `ℓ̃ < ℓ ≤ h`). Here `μ − e_p ≼ μ ≼ μ̃ ≼ μ̃ ⊔ 1`.

*(e2) `ℓ̃ < j ≤ ℓ`: `μ + e_j` against `μ̃ ⊔ 1`.* We need `S_t(μ) + [t ≥ r̲_j] ≤ S_t(μ̃) + [t > ℓ̃]`. For `t > ℓ̃` the right bracket is `1`, so this is immediate. For `t ≤ ℓ̃` with `t ≥ r̲_j` we need strictness. Suppose `t` is tight. Then `r̲_j ≤ t ≤ ℓ̃ < j`, so `μ_t = μ_{t+1} = μ_j ≥ 1`. By (T), `μ̃_t ≤ μ_t = μ_j`, so `μ̃_s ≤ μ_j` for every `s ≥ t`. Moreover `μ̃_j = 0`, since `j > ℓ̃`. Hence `Σ_{s=t+1}^{j} μ̃_s < (j − t)μ_j = Σ_{s=t+1}^{j} μ_s`, and with tightness `S_j(μ̃) < S_j(μ)`, a contradiction.

The positions `1, …, L` are covered by (a)–(e) exactly once: removals occupy positions `≤ ℓ` or `≤ ℓ̃`, additions the last `ℓ` or `ℓ̃` positions, and the middle the rest. The degenerate cases `ℓ = 0`, `ℓ = h` (no middle positions) and `ℓ̃ = h` are included. ∎

### 5.7 (P3) Every tight pattern of a layer lies in the right slice
We need three identities of polynomials, all with integer coefficients, hence valid in `C_m` over any field.

**Lemma 5.7 (identities over `Z`).** *(i) For `0 ≤ t ≤ q−2`: `[y_1^{q−2−t}] D(y_1, y_b) = (−1)^{t+1} y_b^t`.*
*(ii) Let `S = {b_1 < ⋯ < b_r}` and `ε_{b_c} := (−1)^{r+c}`. Then `Σ_{b∈S} ε_b Δ(S ∖ b) y_b^t = 0` for `0 ≤ t ≤ r−2`, and `= Δ(S)` for `t = r−1`.*
*(iii) If `1 ∉ B` and `|B| ≤ q−2`, then `Δ(B ∪ {1}) = Π_{c∈B}(y_c − y_1)·Δ(B)` has `y_1`-degree `|B|` in `C_m`, with top coefficient `(−1)^{|B|}Δ(B)`.*

*Proof.* (i) The coefficient is `(−1)^{q−2−t} y_b^t`, and `q − 2` is odd. (ii) The left side is the expansion, along its last row, of the Vandermonde matrix of `S` with its last row `(y_b^{r−1})` replaced by `(y_b^t)`. For `t ≤ r − 2` two rows coincide. (iii) This is the factorisation of the Vandermonde determinant; `y_1^{|B|} ≠ 0` in `C_m` because `|B| ≤ q − 2`. ∎

**Proposition 5.8 (linear algebra).** *For every `i ∈ {0, …, q−2}`: `V_{Λ_i} ⊆ W_{q−2−i}(V_Λ)`. Here `V_{Λ_i} ⊆ C_{m−1}` is built on the indices `2, …, m`.*

*Proof.* Since `W_{q−2−i}(V_Λ)` is an ideal (Lemma 5.1(i)), it suffices to treat a generator. Let `μ ∈ Λ_i`, so `Φ := F_Λ(μ) ≥ i + 1`, and let `G` be the product of a tight pattern of `μ` on `{2, …, m}`, with pairs `P` and column blocks `B_c`. By Lemma 5.1(ii) it is enough to show `G ∈ W_j(V_Λ)` for some `j ≤ q − 1 − Φ`: then `j ≤ q − 2 − i`. We use (5.1). Since `Φ ≥ 1`, at least one term of (5.1) is non-zero, and we split into three cases, which are mutually exclusive by construction: (α) `j_0` exists; (β) `j_0` does not exist and `ι = 1`; (γ) neither, so that `Φ = r ≥ 1`. In case (β), `ι = 1` forces `ℓ < h`, since `μ ⊔ 1 ∈ Par_m` requires `ℓ(μ) + 1 ≤ h`.

*(α) `j_0` exists (`λ := μ + e_{j_0} ∈ Λ`).* The column `c* := μ_{j_0} + 1` of `μ` has height `j_0 − 1`, because `j_0` is the first row of its length; let `B := B_{c*}` (possibly empty). Put the index `1` into `B`. The result is a tight pattern of `λ` on `[m]`: column `c*` of `λ` has height `j_0`, and the number of pairs `(m − |λ|)/2 = ((m−1) − |μ|)/2` is unchanged. Its product lies in `V_Λ`, has `y_1`-degree `j_0 − 1`, and by Lemma 5.7(iii) its top `y_1`-coefficient is `±G` (note `|B| = j_0 − 1 ≤ h − 1 ≤ q − 2`). So `G ∈ W_{j_0 − 1}(V_Λ)`. By (5.1), `r = ℓ` here. If `ℓ < h`, then `ι = 1` and `Φ = ℓ + (q − 1 − 2ℓ) + (ℓ − j_0 + 1) = q − j_0`. If `ℓ = h`, then `q − 1 − 2ℓ = 0` and `Φ = 2ℓ − j_0 + 1 = q − j_0`. In both cases `j_0 − 1 = q − 1 − Φ`.

*(β) no `j_0`, and `ι = 1` (`λ := μ ⊔ 1 ∈ Λ`, `ℓ < h`).* Put the index `1` into the first column block `B_1`, of height `ℓ`; column 1 of `λ` has height `ℓ + 1`, and the number of pairs is unchanged. The product has `y_1`-degree `ℓ ≤ q − 2` and top coefficient `±G`, so `G ∈ W_ℓ(V_Λ)`. By (5.1), `r = ℓ` and `Φ = q − 1 − ℓ`, i.e. `ℓ = q − 1 − Φ`. (For `μ = ∅` the block is `{1}`, `Δ = 1`, and the degree is `0`.)

*(γ) only removals (`Φ = r ≥ 1`, `λ := μ − e_r ∈ Λ`).* The column `μ_r` of `μ` has height `r`, because `r` is the last row of its length; call its block `S`, `|S| = r`, and write `G = Δ(S)·G'`, where `G'` involves no index of `S ∪ {1}`. For `b ∈ S`, the pairs `P ∪ {(1, b)}` together with the blocks `B_c` (`c ≠ μ_r`) and `S ∖ {b}` form a tight pattern of `λ` on `[m]`: column `μ_r` of `λ` has height `r − 1`, and the number of pairs `(m − |λ|)/2 = ((m−1) − |μ|)/2 + 1` has grown by one. Hence

`f := Σ_{b∈S} ε_b Δ(S ∖ b) D(y_1, y_b) G' ∈ V_Λ`.

By Lemma 5.7(i),(ii), `[y_1^{q−2−t}] f = (−1)^{t+1} (Σ_b ε_b Δ(S∖b) y_b^t) G'`. This vanishes for `t ≤ r − 2` and equals `±Δ(S)G' = ±G` for `t = r − 1`. So `deg_{y_1} f ≤ q − 1 − r`, with top coefficient `±G`, and `G ∈ W_{q−1−r}(V_Λ) = W_{q−1−Φ}(V_Λ)`. ∎

The three constructions land exactly in the slice `q − 1 − Φ`, the largest index the proof can afford (see Remark 5.10(2)).

### 5.8 The induction
*Proof of Theorem 5.3.* Induction on `m`, with `F`, `q` and `T` fixed.
- **Base `m = 0`.** `Par_0 = {∅}`. If `Λ = {∅}`, then `V_Λ = C_0 = F` (the empty product) and `Z_Λ = T^0` is one point: `1 ≥ 1`. If `Λ = ∅`, both sides are `0`.
- **Relabelling.** A permutation of the index set sends every tight-pattern product to `±` another one, since `D` and `Δ` are antisymmetric. Hence `V_Λ` is invariant under relabelling, and so is `|Z_Λ|`. So the statement for `m − 1` variables applies to the variables `y_2, …, y_m`.
- **Step.** Let `m ≥ 1`. By Lemma 5.1(iii), Proposition 5.8, the induction hypothesis, and Lemma 5.1(iv),

`dim V_Λ = Σ_{i=0}^{q−2} dim W_{q−2−i}(V_Λ) ≥ Σ_{i=0}^{q−2} dim V_{Λ_i} ≥ Σ_{i=0}^{q−2} |Z_{Λ_i}| = Σ_{i=0}^{q−2} |Z_{>i}| = |Z_Λ|`.

The induction hypothesis applies because each `Λ_i` is a down-set of `Par_{m−1}` (Proposition 5.6). The identity `Z_{Λ_i} = Z_{>i}` is §5.4. ∎

### 5.9 Equality
For an ideal `I ⊆ F[y_1, …, y_m]` let `gr(I)` be the ideal of top-degree forms: its degree-`d` part consists of `0` and the homogeneous parts of degree `d` of the elements of `I` of degree exactly `d`. (It is an ideal: for `f, g ∈ I` of degree `d`, the form `f^{top} + g^{top}` is `0` or `(f + g)^{top}`, and `y_i f^{top} = (y_i f)^{top}`.)

**Lemma 5.8′ (top-degree forms).** *(i) If `I ⊆ F[y]` is generated by `f_1, …, f_s`, then `gr(I) ⊇ (f_1^{top}, …, f_s^{top})`. (ii) If `dim_F F[y]/I < ∞`, then `dim_F F[y]/gr(I) = dim_F F[y]/I`.*

*Proof.* (i) `f_i ∈ I`, so `f_i^{top} ∈ gr(I)`. (ii) Fix a degree-compatible monomial order. For `f ≠ 0`, the leading monomial of `f` is that of `f^{top}`. The ideal `gr(I)` is homogeneous, so each of its non-zero elements `g` has `g^{top} ∈ gr(I)`, and `g^{top} = f^{top}` for some `f ∈ I`. Hence `gr(I)` and `I` have the same monomial ideal of leading monomials, and by Macaulay's basis theorem [CLO] the standard monomials are a basis of both quotients. ∎

(The initial ideal of `I` is a monomial ideal and contains the leading monomials of the `f_i`, not the `f_i`; it is `gr(I)` that contains the forms themselves. The previous version of this paper wrote «initial ideal» for `gr` in the proofs of Theorem 5.9 and Proposition 9.1.)

**Theorem 5.9.** *If `char F` does not divide `q − 1`, then `dim_F V_Λ = |Z_Λ|` for every `m ≥ 0` and every `≼`-down-set `Λ ⊆ Par_m^{(h)}`. In particular, for such `F`, equality holds in Theorem B (for every field, see Theorem 5.9′), and `dim_{F_p}(D_J : J ∈ 𝒥)C = Q_k(q)` for `q = p^v` without any appeal to topology.*

*Proof.* By Theorem 5.3 it suffices to prove `≤`. The generators of `V_Λ` have integer coefficients, so `dim_F V_Λ` does not change under extension of the field, and we may assume that `F` contains the group `μ_{q−1}` of `(q−1)`-th roots of unity. Since `char F ∤ q − 1`, the polynomial `y^{q−1} − 1` is separable, `|μ_{q−1}| = q − 1`, and `−1 ∈ μ_{q−1}` is not `1` (because `q − 1` is even and `char F ≠ 2`). So we may take `T := μ_{q−1}` with the involution `u ↦ −u`, which has no fixed point.

*Evaluation.* For `a, b ∈ T`, the identity `(a + b)D(a, b) = b^{q−1} − a^{q−1} = 0` shows `D(a, b) = 0` unless `b = −a`, and `D(a, −a) = Σ_u (−1)^u(−1)^{q−2−u}a^{q−2} = −(q−1)a^{q−2} ≠ 0`. A Vandermonde determinant `Δ(B)` is non-zero at a point iff the values on `B` are pairwise distinct. So the product of a tight pattern `(P, B_1, …, B_{λ_1})` of `λ` is non-zero at `M ∈ T^m` iff every pair of `P` is a negation pair of `M` and every block `B_c` carries pairwise distinct values.

*Support.* Suppose such a product is non-zero at `M`. Let `M'` be the multiset of the `|λ|` values of `M` outside `P`, and `ν'` its multiplicity partition. A value occurs at most once in each column block, so the `t` most frequent values of `M'` occupy at most `min(t, λ'_c)` places of column `c`; summing over `c`, `S_t(ν') ≤ Σ_c min(t, λ'_c) = S_t(λ)`, so `ν' ≼ λ`. The pairs of `P` do not change the residue, and passing from `M'` to its residue replaces, in each class, the two multiplicities `a, ā` by one part `|a − ā| ≤ max(a, ā)`; so the parts of `λ(M)` are bounded, one by one, by distinct parts of `ν'`, and `λ(M) ≼ ν'`. Hence `λ(M) ≼ λ`, and if `λ ∈ Λ` then `M ∈ Z_Λ`. So the union `Σ` of the supports of the generators of `V_Λ` is contained in `Z_Λ`. (It is in fact equal: for `M ∈ Z_Λ`, take `ν(M)` disjoint negation pairs of `M` and put the residue into the columns of `λ(M)`, one value per row.)

*Degeneration.* Let `g_1, …, g_s` be the generators of `V_Λ`, viewed as homogeneous polynomials in `F[y_1, …, y_m]`, and put `I := (g_1, …, g_s) + (y_1^{q−1} − 1, …, y_m^{q−1} − 1)`. Since `y^{q−1} − 1 = Π_{u∈T}(y − u)`, the Chinese remainder theorem gives `F[y]/(y_i^{q−1} − 1) ≅ F^{T^m}`, the ring of functions on `T^m`. In a finite product of fields, the ideal generated by some functions consists of the functions that vanish outside the union of their supports. So `I` is the ideal of the functions vanishing on `T^m ∖ Σ`, and `dim_F F[y]/I = (q−1)^m − |Σ|`. The top-degree form of `g_i` is `g_i` and that of `y_i^{q−1} − 1` is `y_i^{q−1}`. So, by Lemma 5.8′(i), `I' := (g_1, …, g_s) + (y_1^{q−1}, …, y_m^{q−1}) ⊆ gr(I)`, and by Lemma 5.8′(ii)

`dim_F C_m/V_Λ = dim_F F[y]/I' ≥ dim_F F[y]/gr(I) = dim_F F[y]/I = (q−1)^m − |Σ|`,

that is, `dim_F V_Λ ≤ |Σ| ≤ |Z_Λ|`. ∎

*Complement (the associated graded ring).* Assume, as in the proof, that `char F ∤ q − 1` and `μ_{q−1} ⊆ F`, and read `V_Λ + (y_i^{q−1})` as the ideal `(g_i) + (y_i^{q−1})` of `F[y_1, …, y_m]`. In the situation of the proof, Theorem 5.3 turns every inequality into an equality: `Σ = Z_Λ` and `I' = gr(I)`. So `V_Λ + (y_1^{q−1}, …, y_m^{q−1})` is the ideal of top-degree forms of the ideal of the finite set `T^m ∖ Z_Λ ⊆ μ_{q−1}^m`, and `C_m/V_Λ` is the associated graded ring of the ring of functions on `T^m ∖ Z_Λ`, for the degree filtration. In other words, the tight-pattern products give a complete description of this associated graded ring. This is the same kind of statement as the Garsia–Procesi and Tanisaki descriptions of the associated graded rings of orbits [GP], [Ta], here for a new family of point sets, indexed by down-sets of partitions under weak dominance.

**Theorem 5.9′ (equality in every characteristic; freeness over `Z`).** *For every field `F`, every odd `q ≥ 3`, every `m ≥ 0` and every `≼`-down-set `Λ ⊆ Par_m^{(h)}`, `dim_F V_Λ = |Z_Λ|`. Moreover, let `V_{Λ,Z}` be the ideal of `C_{m,Z} := Z[y_1, …, y_m]/(y_1^{q−1}, …, y_m^{q−1})` generated by the tight-pattern products of the elements of `Λ`. Then `C_{m,Z}/V_{Λ,Z}` is a free abelian group of rank `(q−1)^m − |Z_Λ|`, each of its graded pieces is free, and the dimension over `F` of each graded piece of `V_Λ` does not depend on `F`.*

*Proof.* The ring `C_{m,Z}` is free of rank `(q−1)^m` on the monomials, and `V_{Λ,Z}` is homogeneous, so `M := C_{m,Z}/V_{Λ,Z} = ⊕_d M_d` with each `M_d` a finitely generated abelian group. The image of `V_{Λ,Z}` in `C_{m,Z} ⊗ F = C_m` generates `V_Λ` (the generators have integer coefficients), so by right exactness of `⊗`, `C_m/V_Λ ≅ M ⊗ F`, degree by degree. Write `M_d ≅ Z^{ρ_d} ⊕ (finite)`. Then `dim_F(M_d ⊗ F) = ρ_d + c_d(F)`, where `c_d(F)` is the number of cyclic factors of order divisible by `char F` in the finite part (`c_d(F) = 0` if `char F = 0`). With `F = Q`, where Theorem 5.9 applies because `0` does not divide `q − 1`, `Σ_d ρ_d = dim_Q C_{m,Q}/V_{Λ,Q} = (q−1)^m − |Z_Λ|`. So for every field `F`, `dim_F C_m/V_Λ ≥ (q−1)^m − |Z_Λ|`, that is, `dim_F V_Λ ≤ |Z_Λ|`, and Theorem 5.3 gives equality. Equality forces `c_d(F) = 0` for every `d`. Taking `F = F_p` for every prime `p`, no `M_d` has torsion, so `M` is free and `dim_F (C_m/V_Λ)_d = ρ_d` for every `F`. ∎

**Remark 5.10.** (1) *Where the full family enters.* Only at the root, Lemma 5.2. The down-set closure of `{(1)}` then carries the symmetry of `𝒥` through every layer. §9 shows that no argument valid for all subfamilies can exist. (2) *No slack.* In Proposition 5.8 the slice `q − 1 − F` cannot be lowered: replacing it by `q − 2 − F` makes the statement false (§12, negative controls). (3) *The characteristic.* Theorem 5.3 and, by Theorem 5.9′, equality hold over every field. Theorem 5.9 is needed only over `Q`. By the Complement to Theorem 5.9, the Hilbert function part of this statement holds in general: the graded dimensions of `C_m/V_Λ` are those of the degree filtration on the functions on `T^m ∖ Z_Λ`.

## 6. Odd degrees that are not prime powers: the colour reduction

In §6–§7, `m ≥ 3` is odd and `p` is a prime dividing `m`. Write `m = q·r` with `q = p^v`, `v ≥ 1`, and `p ∤ r`; so `r` is odd, and `r = 1` is the case treated in §2–§5. Put `F := F̄_p`, `n = 2k` with `k ≥ 1`, `N = n + 2`,

`F[G] := F[t_1, …, t_{n+1}]/(t_1^m − 1, …, t_{n+1}^m − 1)`,   `I := (ψ̄_J : J ∈ 𝒥)·F[G]`,

where `ψ̄_J` denotes the image of `ψ_J` in `F[G]` (in §2 it denoted the image in `F_p[G]`; the ideal of `F[G]` it generates is the extension of that ideal).

By Proposition 2.1, `dim_{F_p} F_p[G]/(ψ̄_J : J ∈ 𝒥) ≥ m^{n+1} − |Γ_𝒥|` for every `p`, with equality at every `p | m` if and only if `H_n(X)/L(X)` is torsion free. The `ψ̄_J` have coefficients in `F_p`, so `(ψ̄_J)F[G] = (ψ̄_J)F_p[G] ⊗_{F_p} F`, and dimensions do not change under this extension of the field; so

`dim_F I ≤ |Γ_𝒥|`,   (6.1)

and **the Main Theorem at `m` is equivalent to `dim_F I ≥ |Γ_𝒥|` for every prime `p | m`.** This section reduces that inequality to an inequality for one family of ideals in two sets of variables, the bipartite ideals; §7 proves it. All tensor products are over `F`, and a «unit» is an invertible element of the ring in which the statement is made. The idea is that `F[G]` is a product of local rings indexed by the ways of colouring the coordinates with `r`-th roots of unity; on each factor the generators `ψ̄_J` either vanish or become pure tensors, and the pure tensors split along the colour classes.

### 6.1 The splitting
**Lemma 6.1 (splitting).** *Put `R_c := ⊗_{i=1}^{n+1} F[t_i]/((t_i − c_i)^q)` for `c = (c_1, …, c_{n+1}) ∈ μ_r^{n+1}`, where `μ_r ⊆ F^×` is the group of `r`-th roots of unity. Then the product of the quotient maps `π_c : F[G] → R_c` is an isomorphism `F[G] ≅ Π_{c ∈ μ_r^{n+1}} R_c`, and for every ideal `I' ⊆ F[G]`, `dim_F I' = Σ_c dim_F π_c(I')`, where `π_c(I')` is the ideal of `R_c` generated by the images of any set of generators of `I'`.*

*Proof.* In characteristic `p`, `(t^r − 1)^q = t^m − 1`. The polynomial `t^r − 1` is separable, since `r ≠ 0` in `F`, so `t^r − 1 = Π_{ζ ∈ μ_r}(t − ζ)` with `r` distinct roots, and `t^m − 1 = Π_ζ (t − ζ)^q`. The ideals `((t − ζ)^q)` are pairwise comaximal, and the Chinese remainder theorem gives `F[t]/(t^m − 1) ≅ Π_ζ F[t]/((t − ζ)^q)`. Now `F[G]` is the tensor product of the `n + 1` one-variable rings, and the tensor product over a field distributes over finite products of algebras. In a finite product of rings every ideal is the product of its images, which are generated by the images of its generators; dimensions add. ∎

### 6.2 Which generators survive
Fix `c ∈ μ_r^{n+1}` and put `c_0 := (c_1 ⋯ c_{n+1})^{−1}`, so that `Π_{i=0}^{n+1} c_i = 1`. In `R_c` every `t_i − c_i` is nilpotent, with `(t_i − c_i)^q = 0`.

**Lemma 6.2.** *(i) For `1 ≤ i ≤ n+1`: if `c_i ≠ 1`, then `t_i − 1` is a unit of `R_c`; if `c_i = 1`, it is nilpotent.*
*(ii) Let `1 ≤ j < l ≤ n+1` and `u := t_jt_l ∈ R_c`. If `c_jc_l ≠ 1`, then `φ(u) = 0` in `R_c`. If `c_jc_l = 1`, then `φ(u) = (unit)·(u − 1)^{q−1}`.*
*(iii) If some pair `{j_i, k_i}` of `J`, `i ≥ 1`, has `c_{j_i}c_{k_i} ≠ 1`, then `π_c(ψ̄_J) = 0`. If `c_{j_i}c_{k_i} = 1` for `i = 1, …, k`, then also `c_0c_{k_0} = 1`, and*

`π_c(ψ̄_J) = (unit)·(t_{k_0} − 1)·Π_{i=1}^{k} (t_{k_i} − 1)(t_{j_i}t_{k_i} − 1)^{q−1}`.   (6.2)

*Proof.* (i) `t_i − 1 = (c_i − 1) + (t_i − c_i)`, a non-zero constant plus a nilpotent if `c_i ≠ 1`. (ii) In `F[u]`, `(u − 1)φ(u) = u^m − 1 = Π_{ξ ∈ μ_r}(u − ξ)^q`, so `φ(u) = (u − 1)^{q−1}·Π_{ξ ≠ 1}(u − ξ)^q`. In `R_c`, `t_j^q = c_j^q` and `t_l^q = c_l^q` (Frobenius), so `(u − c_jc_l)^q = 0`. If `ξ_0 := c_jc_l ≠ 1`, then `ξ_0 ∈ μ_r ∖ {1}` and the factor `(u − ξ_0)^q` vanishes. If `c_jc_l = 1`, then `u − 1` is nilpotent and each `u − ξ = (1 − ξ) + (u − 1)`, `ξ ≠ 1`, is a unit. (iii) The first sentence follows from (ii). For the second, `1 = Π_{i=0}^{n+1} c_i = c_0c_{k_0}·Π_{i≥1} c_{j_i}c_{k_i}`; then apply (ii) to each factor `φ(t_{j_i}t_{k_i})`. ∎

### 6.3 Colour classes and the tensor decomposition
For `ζ ∈ μ_r` put `𝒞_ζ := {i ∈ {0, …, n+1} : c_i = ζ}` (the index `0` carries the colour `c_0`). Since `r` is odd, `ζ ≠ ζ^{−1}` for `ζ ≠ 1`, and `μ_r ∖ {1}` splits into `(r − 1)/2` pairs `{ζ, ζ^{−1}}`; fix a set `ℛ_r` of representatives. The sets `𝒞_1` and `𝒞_ζ ∪ 𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}` (`ζ ∈ ℛ_r`) partition `{0, …, n+1}`; we call them the **blocks** of `c`. We say that `J ∈ 𝒥` is **compatible** with `c` if `c_ac_b = 1` for every pair `{a, b}` of `J`; by Lemma 6.2(iii) this means `c_{j_i}c_{k_i} = 1` for `i = 1, …, k`, and `π_c(ψ̄_J) = 0` unless `J` is compatible.

**Lemma 6.3.** *A pair `{a, b}` has `c_ac_b = 1` iff `{a, b} ⊆ 𝒞_1`, or `{a, b}` meets both `𝒞_ζ` and `𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}` for some `ζ ∈ ℛ_r`. Hence `J` is compatible with `c` iff it consists of a perfect matching `P` of `𝒞_1` and, for each `ζ ∈ ℛ_r`, of the pairs of a bijection `σ_ζ : 𝒞_ζ → 𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}`; and every such choice `(P, (σ_ζ))` is a compatible matching (it is a perfect matching of `{0, …, n+1}`, hence an element of `𝒥`: this is where the full family is used, cf. Remark 6.10(3)). A compatible matching exists iff `|𝒞_1|` is even and `|𝒞_ζ| = |𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}|` for every `ζ`; we then call `c` a **compatible colouring**.* ∎

Group the tensor factors of `R_c` by blocks: `R_c = R_{c,1} ⊗ ⊗_{ζ ∈ ℛ_r} R_{c,ζ}`, where `R_{c,1} := ⊗_{i ∈ 𝒞_1 ∖ {0}} F[t_i]/((t_i − 1)^q)` and `R_{c,ζ} := ⊗_{i ∈ (𝒞_ζ ∪ 𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}) ∖ {0}} F[t_i]/((t_i − c_i)^q)` (an empty tensor product is `F`; the variable `t_0` does not exist). Each factor of (6.2) involves the variables of one pair of `J`, and a pair of a compatible `J` lies in one block; the factor of the pair `{0, k_0}` is `t_{k_0} − 1`, which lies in the block of `k_0`. So, for a compatible `J = (P, (σ_ζ))`,

`π_c(ψ̄_J) = (unit)·g_P ⊗ ⊗_{ζ ∈ ℛ_r} g_{σ_ζ}`,

where `g_P ∈ R_{c,1}` and `g_{σ_ζ} ∈ R_{c,ζ}` are the products of the factors of (6.2) belonging to the pairs of `P` and of `σ_ζ` (an empty product is `1`; the unit of (6.2) is absorbed into one of them, and units do not change the ideals below). Put `I_{c,1} := (g_P : P)` and `I_{c,ζ} := (g_σ : σ)`, the ideals generated over all perfect matchings `P` of `𝒞_1` and all bijections `σ : 𝒞_ζ → 𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}`.

**Lemma 6.4 (tensor products of ideals).** *Let `R, R'` be finite-dimensional commutative `F`-algebras and `I = (g_α)`, `I' = (g'_β)` ideals. Then the ideal of `R ⊗ R'` generated by the `g_α ⊗ g'_β` is `I ⊗ I'`, and `dim(I ⊗ I') = dim I · dim I'`.*

*Proof.* `I ⊗ I'` is an ideal of `R ⊗ R'` (pure tensors span, and `(r ⊗ r')(x ⊗ x') = rx ⊗ r'x'`), and it contains the `g_α ⊗ g'_β`. Conversely, for `x = Σ_α r_α g_α ∈ I` and `x' = Σ_β r'_β g'_β ∈ I'`, `x ⊗ x' = Σ_{α,β}(r_α ⊗ r'_β)(g_α ⊗ g'_β)`. A basis of `I` tensored with a basis of `I'` is a basis of `I ⊗ I'`. ∎

**Proposition 6.5 (tensor decomposition).** *If `c` is not compatible, `π_c(I) = 0`. If `c` is compatible, `π_c(I) = I_{c,1} ⊗ ⊗_{ζ ∈ ℛ_r} I_{c,ζ}` and `dim π_c(I) = dim I_{c,1}·Π_ζ dim I_{c,ζ}`.*

*Proof.* Units do not change the ideal generated, so by Lemmas 6.2 and 6.3 `π_c(I)` is generated by the pure tensors `g_P ⊗ ⊗_ζ g_{σ_ζ}`, over all independent choices of `P` and `(σ_ζ)`. Apply Lemma 6.4 repeatedly. ∎

### 6.4 The block of colour `1`
In `F[t]/((t − 1)^q)` put `y := t − t^{−1}`. Lemma 2.3 holds verbatim here: its proof uses only `(t − 1)^q = 0`, `q = p^v` and `2 ∈ F^×`. So `R_{c,1} = F[y_i : i ∈ 𝒞_1 ∖ {0}]/(y_i^q)`, with `y_i = (unit)·(t_i − 1)` and `t_it_l − 1 = (unit)·(y_i + y_l)`. By Lemma 2.4(i), for a pair `{i < l} ⊆ 𝒞_1 ∖ {0}`, `(t_l − 1)(t_it_l − 1)^{q−1} = (unit)·y_iy_l·D(y_i, y_l)`; if `0 ∈ 𝒞_1`, the pair `{0, k_0}` contributes `t_{k_0} − 1 = (unit)·y_{k_0}`. The indices in these monomial factors are the elements of `𝒞_1 ∖ {0}`, each once, so `g_P = (unit)·Y_1·D_P` with `Y_1 := Π_{i ∈ 𝒞_1 ∖ 0} y_i` and `D_P` the product of the `D(y_i, y_l)` over the pairs of `P` avoiding `0`. By the argument of Proposition 2.5 (the kernel of multiplication by `Y_1` on `R_{c,1}` is exactly `(y_i^{q−1} : i ∈ 𝒞_1 ∖ 0)`, so `f ↦ Y_1f` induces an injective map from the quotient `C(𝒞_1 ∖ 0)` into `R_{c,1}`),

`dim I_{c,1} = dim_F (D_P : P)·C(𝒞_1 ∖ 0)`,   `C(S) := F[y_i : i ∈ S]/(y_i^{q−1} : i ∈ S)`.

Let `N_1(c)` be the number of tuples `(w_i)_{i ∈ 𝒞_1 ∖ 0} ∈ (μ_q ∖ {1})^{𝒞_1 ∖ 0}` such that, with `w_0 := (Π w_i)^{−1}` if `0 ∈ 𝒞_1`, the multiset `{w_i : i ∈ 𝒞_1}` lies in `μ_q ∖ {1}` and is closed under inversion. (For `|𝒞_1|` even the condition `w_0 ≠ 1` is automatic: otherwise the other `|𝒞_1| − 1` entries, an odd number, would form a multiset in `μ_q ∖ {1}` closed under the fixed-point-free inversion.)

**Lemma 6.6.** *`dim I_{c,1} ≥ N_1(c)`.*

*Proof.* If `|𝒞_1|` is odd, both sides are `0`: there is no perfect matching, and a multiset closed under the fixed-point-free inversion of `μ_q ∖ {1}` has even size. Let `|𝒞_1| = 2k'`.
- If `0 ∈ 𝒞_1`, relabel `𝒞_1 ∖ {0}` increasingly as `{1, …, 2k' − 1}`. Write `𝒥_j` for the set `𝒥` in dimension `2j`. The perfect matchings `P` of `𝒞_1` are the matchings `J ∈ 𝒥_{k'−1}`, and `D_P = ±D_J`. So `dim I_{c,1} = dim(D_J : J ∈ 𝒥_{k'−1})C_{2k'−1} ≥ Q_{k'−1}(q)` by Lemma 5.2 and Theorem 5.3 (over every field, here `F`). By Lemma 2.2 (whose proof uses only that `q` is odd), `N_1(c) = Q_{k'−1}(q)`. (For `k' = 1` both sides are `q − 1`.)
- If `0 ∉ 𝒞_1`, relabel `𝒞_1` as `{1, …, 2k'}`. In `Par_{2k'}^{(h)}` the set `{∅}` is a down-set, since `λ ≼ ∅` forces `λ = ∅`. A tight pattern of `∅` on `[2k']` has `k'` pairs and no blocks: it is a perfect matching `P`, with product `D_P`. So `(D_P : P)C_{2k'} = V_{{∅}}`, and Theorem 5.3 gives `dim ≥ |Z_{{∅}}|`, the number of `2k'`-tuples in `T` whose multiset is closed under the involution. A bijection `μ_q ∖ {1} → T` carrying inversion to `u ↦ −u` (both are sets of `q − 1` elements with a fixed-point-free involution) identifies this number with `N_1(c)`. (For `k' = 0`, `I_{c,1} = R_{c,1} = F` and `N_1(c) = 1`.) ∎

### 6.5 The pair blocks
Fix `ζ ∈ ℛ_r`, and put `A := 𝒞_ζ`, `B := 𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}`, `A^* := A ∖ {0}`, `B^* := B ∖ {0}`. For `i ∈ A^*` put `x_i := ζ^{−1}t_i − 1`, and for `l ∈ B^*` put `z_l := (ζt_l)^{−1} − 1`.

**Lemma 6.7.** *(i) `R_{c,ζ} = F[x_i (i ∈ A^*), z_l (l ∈ B^*)]/(x_i^q, z_l^q)`. (ii) For `i ∈ A^*`, `l ∈ B^*`: `t_it_l − 1 = (unit)·(x_i − z_l)`. (iii) Let `α := |A^*|` and `β := |B^*|` (the numbers of `x`- and `z`-variables, as in §7), and relabel `A^* = {1, …, α}`, `B^* = {1, …, β}`. If `0 ∉ A ∪ B` and `α = β =: a`, then `I_{c,ζ} ≅ I^{bal}_a := (Π_{i=1}^{a}(x_i − z_{σ(i)})^{q−1} : σ ∈ S_a)`. If `0 ∈ B` and `α = β + 1`, then `I_{c,ζ} ≅ I^{ph}_β`, where `I^{ph}_a := (Π_{i ≠ i_0}(x_i − z_{σ(i)})^{q−1} : i_0 ∈ [a+1], σ : [a+1] ∖ {i_0} → [a] bijective)`; if `0 ∈ A` and `β = α + 1`, then `I_{c,ζ} ≅ I^{ph}_α`, after exchanging the names `x` and `z`. In all other cases `I_{c,ζ} = 0`.*

*Proof.* (i) `x_i = ζ^{−1}(t_i − ζ)` is a unit multiple of the generator of the maximal ideal of the local ring `F[t_i]/((t_i − ζ)^q)`, so `x_i^q = 0` and `1, x_i, …, x_i^{q−1}` is a basis. Similarly `ε_l := ζt_l − 1` is nilpotent with `ε_l^q = 0`, and `z_l = (1 + ε_l)^{−1} − 1 = −ε_l(1 + ε_l)^{−1}` is a unit multiple of `ε_l`. (ii) `t_i = ζ(1 + x_i)` and `t_l = ζ^{−1}(1 + z_l)^{−1}`, so `t_it_l − 1 = (x_i − z_l)(1 + z_l)^{−1}`. (iii) Every `t_s − 1` with `s ∈ A^* ∪ B^*` is a unit (Lemma 6.2(i)), since `c_s ≠ 1`. By (6.2) and (ii), the factor of a pair `{i, l}`, `i ∈ A^*`, `l ∈ B^*`, is `(unit)·(x_i − z_l)^{q−1}`, and the factor of the pair `{0, k_0}` is a unit. If `0 ∉ A ∪ B`, the bijections `A → B` are the `σ ∈ S_a`, `a = α = β`. If `0 ∈ B`, a bijection `σ : A → B` sends exactly one `i_0 ∈ A` to `0`, and the other pairs give the product over `i ≠ i_0`. If `0 ∈ A`, the same holds with the roles of `A` and `B` exchanged; since `q − 1` is even, `(x − z)^{q−1} = (z − x)^{q−1}`, so exchanging the names of the variables carries this ideal onto `I^{ph}_a`. If `|A| ≠ |B|`, there is no bijection. (For `α = β = 0`, `I^{bal}_0 = (1)` is the ring `F`; for `β = 0` in the second case, `I^{ph}_0 = (1)` is the ring `F[x_1]/(x_1^q)`; and in the third case with `α = 0`, for instance `𝒞_ζ = {0}` and `𝒞_{ζ^{−1}} = {l}`, it is the ring `F[z_1]/(z_1^q)`, of dimension `q`.) ∎

### 6.6 The count factors in the same way
Let `Ω` be a set of `q` elements. For `a ≥ 0` put

`N_{bal}(a, q) := #{(ξ, η) ∈ Ω^a × Ω^a : the multisets of ξ and η are equal} = Σ_{c_1 + ⋯ + c_q = a} (a!/(c_1!⋯c_q!))^2`,

`N_{ph}(a, q) := #{(ξ, η) ∈ Ω^{a+1} × Ω^a : the multiset of η is contained in that of ξ}`.

**Lemma 6.8.** *`|Γ_𝒥| = Σ_c N_1(c)·Π_{ζ ∈ ℛ_r} N_ζ(c)`, the sum over the compatible colourings, where `N_ζ(c) := N_{bal}(α, q)` in the first case of Lemma 6.7(iii) and `N_ζ(c) := N_{ph}(min(α, β), q)` in the other two (`α`, `β` as there).*

*Proof.* By the first paragraph of the proof of Lemma 2.2 (which uses nothing about `m`), `Γ_𝒥` is the set of `g = (g_1, …, g_{n+1}) ∈ μ_m^{n+1}` such that, with `g_0 := (g_1 ⋯ g_{n+1})^{−1}`, all `g_i ≠ 1` and the multiset `{g_0, …, g_{n+1}}` splits into inverse pairs; since `m` is odd, inversion has no fixed point on `μ_m ∖ {1}`, and «splits into inverse pairs» means «closed under inversion». Here `μ_m ⊂ C` is the group of complex `m`-th roots of unity (the rest of §6 works over `F̄_p`). As `gcd(q, r) = 1`, `μ_m = μ_q × μ_r`; write `g_i = c_iw_i` with `c_i ∈ μ_r`, `w_i ∈ μ_q` (`i = 0, …, n+1`). Then `c_0 = (c_1 ⋯ c_{n+1})^{−1}`, `w_0 = (w_1 ⋯ w_{n+1})^{−1}`, `g_ig_l = 1` iff `c_ic_l = 1` and `w_iw_l = 1`, and `g_i ≠ 1` iff `(c_i, w_i) ≠ (1, 1)`. If `g ∈ Γ_𝒥`, its pairing into inverse pairs is a matching compatible with `c := (c_1, …, c_{n+1})`, so `c` is a compatible colouring. Fix a compatible `c`. By Lemma 6.3, a tuple `g` with colouring `c` lies in `Γ_𝒥` iff (1) the multiset `{w_i : i ∈ 𝒞_1}` lies in `μ_q ∖ {1}` and is closed under inversion, and (2) for each `ζ ∈ ℛ_r` the multiset `{w_i : i ∈ 𝒞_ζ}` equals the multiset `{w_l^{−1} : l ∈ 𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}}`. (Condition (2) allows the value `w_i = 1`, rightly: for `i ∈ 𝒞_ζ ∪ 𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}` one has `g_i ≠ 1` whatever `w_i` is, since `c_i ≠ 1`.) Each condition involves the `w_i` of one block. The only coupling between blocks is `w_0`: every block that satisfies its condition and does not contain `0` has `Π w_i = 1` over its indices, so, when all these blocks satisfy their conditions, `w_0 = (Π_{i ∈ 𝒦 ∖ 0} w_i)^{−1}` for the block `𝒦 ∋ 0`, a function of the `w_i` of `𝒦` alone. Conversely, given `w`-values on each block that satisfy its condition, where for the block `𝒦 ∋ 0` the value `w_0` is computed from that block alone, the blocks not containing `0` have product `1`, so this value is the true `(w_1 ⋯ w_{n+1})^{−1}` and the assembled tuple lies in `Γ_𝒥`. Hence the count over `c` is the product of the block counts. (For a compatible `c`, exactly one of the three cases of Lemma 6.7(iii) holds for each `ζ`.) The block `𝒞_1` gives `N_1(c)`, and a balanced pair block gives `N_{bal}(α, q)` (`Ω = μ_q`, `ξ := (w_i)_{i ∈ 𝒞_ζ}`, `η := (w_l^{−1})_{l ∈ 𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}}`). For a pair block with `0 ∈ 𝒞_{ζ^{−1}}`, let `ξ` be the `w_i` on `𝒞_ζ` (`β + 1` of them) and `η` the `w_l^{−1}` on `𝒞_{ζ^{−1}} ∖ 0`; the condition says `multiset(ξ) = multiset(η) ∪ {w_0^{−1}}`, with `w_0^{−1} = Π ξ·(Π η)^{−1}`. If `multiset(η) ⊆ multiset(ξ)`, the difference is one element `ξ^*` and `Π ξ·(Π η)^{−1} = ξ^*`, so the condition holds; conversely it implies `multiset(η) ⊆ multiset(ξ)`. So this block gives `N_{ph}(β, q)`, and so does the case `0 ∈ 𝒞_ζ`, by the symmetry `ξ ↔ η`, `w ↦ w^{−1}`. ∎

### 6.7 The reduction
**Proposition 6.9 (colour reduction).** *Let `k ≥ 1`, let `m = q·r` be odd, `q = p^v`, `p ∤ r`, and suppose that `dim_F I^{bal}_a ≥ N_{bal}(a, q)` and `dim_F I^{ph}_a ≥ N_{ph}(a, q)` for every `a ≥ 0`, where `F = F̄_p`. Then `dim_{F_p} F_p[G]/(ψ̄_J : J ∈ 𝒥) = m^{n+1} − |Γ_𝒥|`, so `H_n(X)/L(X)` has no `p`-torsion.*

*Proof.* By Lemma 6.1, Proposition 6.5, Lemmas 6.6 and 6.7 and the hypothesis, `dim_F I = Σ_c dim π_c(I) ≥ Σ_c N_1(c)·Π_ζ N_ζ(c)`, the sum over the compatible colourings, and this is `|Γ_𝒥|` by Lemma 6.8. With (6.1), `dim_F I = |Γ_𝒥|`, and the per-prime clause of Proposition 2.1 concludes. ∎

*Remarks 6.10.* (1) Only the inequality `≥` is needed on each block; the reverse inequality is global, (6.1). (2) For `r = 1` there is one colouring, `𝒞_1 = {0, …, n+1}`, and Proposition 6.9 is Corollary 5.4. (3) For a subfamily `K ⊊ 𝒥` the splitting of Lemma 6.1 still holds, but the matchings of `K` compatible with `c` are no longer a product set, and Proposition 6.5 fails; this is consistent with §9. (4) The splitting of Lemma 6.1 over `F_p`, with two factors per variable (the `(t − 1)`-primary part and the rest), was used earlier by the author as a memory-saving device for machine computations of these ranks, and to prove that any torsion is confined to one block [Rep, *The Localization Theorem*, *The Block Decomposition*]. Proposition 6.9 refines it over `F̄_p` to one factor per colouring and identifies every factor.

**Example 6.11 (`k = 1`, `m = 15`).** Here `N = 4`, `𝒥` has three matchings, and `Q_1(15) = 3·14·13 = 546`. The compatible colourings, grouped by the shape of their blocks, contribute as follows (`i, l, s` are indices other than `0`; `Q_0(q) = q − 1`):

| blocks of the colouring | `p = 3` (`q = 3`, `r = 5`): colourings × product | `p = 5` (`q = 5`, `r = 3`): colourings × product |
|---|---|---|
| `𝒞_1 = {0, 1, 2, 3}` | `1 × Q_1(3) = 6` | `1 × Q_1(5) = 36` |
| `𝒞_1 = {0, i}`, and one balanced block `1 + 1` | `12 × Q_0(3)·N_{bal}(1, 3) = 12 × 6` | `6 × Q_0(5)·N_{bal}(1, 5) = 6 × 20` |
| `𝒞_1 = {i, l}`, and a phantom block `{0}` against `{s}` | `12 × Q_0(3)·N_{ph}(0, 3) = 12 × 6` | `6 × Q_0(5)·N_{ph}(0, 5) = 6 × 20` |
| `𝒞_1 = ∅`, and one phantom block `2 + 2` containing `0` | `12 × N_{ph}(1, 3) = 12 × 15` | `6 × N_{ph}(1, 5) = 6 × 45` |
| `𝒞_1 = ∅`, a balanced block `1 + 1` and a phantom block `{0}` against `{s}` | `24 × N_{bal}(1, 3)·N_{ph}(0, 3) = 24 × 9` | none (`μ_3` has one pair of inverse colours) |
| **total** | **`61` colourings, `546`** | **`19` colourings, `546`** |

At both primes the sum is `Q_1(15)`, as Lemma 6.8 says; the literal computation of §12.3 gives the same `546`. (At `k = 1` the Main Theorem is Degtyarev's theorem [De14]; the example only shows the bookkeeping.)

## 7. The bipartite count

This section proves the hypothesis of Proposition 6.9, and so completes the proof of the Main Theorem. The method is that of §5, with two changes: the box is `q` instead of `q − 1`, and the single partition of §5 is replaced by a *pair* of partitions, one for each of the two sets of variables. Throughout, `q ≥ 3` is odd and `F` is a field in which the binomial coefficients `C(q − 1, t)`, `0 ≤ t ≤ q − 1`, are non-zero: for instance `char F = 0`, or `char F = p` and `q = p^v` (then `C(q−1, t) ≡ (−1)^t (mod p)`, because `(1 + u)^{q−1} = (1 + u^q)(1 + u)^{−1}` in `F_p[[u]]`). Let `Ω` be a set of `q` elements. For finite index sets `A` and `B` put `α := |A|`, `β := |B|` and

`R_{A,B} := F[x_i (i ∈ A), z_l (l ∈ B)]/(x_i^q, z_l^q)`.

(The symbols `A`, `B`, `R_{A,B}` are local to this section.) Points are pairs `(ξ, η) ∈ Ω^A × Ω^B`.

> **THEOREM C (the bipartite count).** *For every `a ≥ 0`:*
>
> `dim_F I^{bal}_a ≥ N_{bal}(a, q)`   *and*   `dim_F I^{ph}_a ≥ N_{ph}(a, q) = N_{bal}(a + 1, q)`,
>
> *where `I^{bal}_a ⊆ R_{[a],[a]}` and `I^{ph}_a ⊆ R_{[a+1],[a]}` are the ideals of Lemma 6.7(iii) and `N_{bal}`, `N_{ph}` are the counts of §6.6.*

Theorem C is the root case of Theorem 7.6 below. With Proposition 6.9 it gives the Main Theorem for `m` not a prime power (Corollary 7.7). Over `F̄_p` both inequalities are equalities (Corollary 7.8); in characteristic `0` they are strict in general (Remarks 7.9).

### 7.1 Peeling with the box `q`
Write `R_{A,B} = R_{A ∖ {i_1}, B}[w]/(w^q)` with `w := x_{i_1}` for some `i_1 ∈ A`. For an ideal `V ⊆ R_{A,B}` and `0 ≤ j ≤ q − 1` put `V_{≤j} := {f ∈ V : deg_w f ≤ j}` and `W_j(V) := {[w^j]f : f ∈ V_{≤j}} ⊆ R_{A ∖ {i_1}, B}`.

**Lemma 7.1 (peeling).** *(i) `W_j(V)` is an ideal. (ii) `W_j(V) ⊆ W_{j+1}(V)` for `0 ≤ j < q − 1`. (iii) `dim V = Σ_{j=0}^{q−1} dim W_j(V)`. (iv) For `Z ⊆ Ω^A × Ω^B` and a tail `M'` (the coordinates other than `i_1`) let `F(M') := {u ∈ Ω : (u, M') ∈ Z}` and `Z_{>i} := {M' : |F(M')| > i}`; then `|Z| = Σ_{i=0}^{q−1} |Z_{>i}|`.*

*Proof.* As for Lemma 5.1, with `q` in place of `q − 1`: in (ii) one uses `w^{j+1} ≠ 0`, i.e. `j + 1 ≤ q − 1`, and in (iv) `|F(M')| ≤ q`. ∎

### 7.2 Pairs of partitions and the bipartite down-set family
We use the partial sums `S_t` and the weak dominance order `≼` of §5.2 (defined also between partitions of different sizes), and the rows `r̄_j`, `r̲_j` of §5.5.
- **Residue shape.** For `(ξ, η)`, let `X` and `Y` be the multisets of values of `ξ` and `η`. Let `R_+` keep, for each value `u` with `mult_X(u) > mult_Y(u)`, the difference `mult_X(u) − mult_Y(u)` of copies of `u`, and let `R_−` be defined symmetrically. The **shape** of `(ξ, η)` is `λ(ξ, η) := (λ_+, λ_−)`, the multiplicity partitions of `R_+` and `R_−`. Their supports are disjoint subsets of `Ω`, so `ℓ(λ_+) + ℓ(λ_−) ≤ q`; moreover `|λ_+| − |λ_−| = α − β` and `|λ_+| + |λ_−| ≤ α + β`.
- **`BPar_q(α, β)`** is the set of pairs `(λ_+, λ_−)` of partitions with `|λ_+| − |λ_−| = α − β`, `|λ_+| + |λ_−| ≤ α + β` and `ℓ(λ_+) + ℓ(λ_−) ≤ q`. It is ordered by the product order: `(λ_+, λ_−) ≼ (μ_+, μ_−)` iff `λ_+ ≼ μ_+` and `λ_− ≼ μ_−`. Every element of `BPar_q(α, β)` is the shape of some point (give the `ℓ(λ_+) + ℓ(λ_−) ≤ q` rows pairwise distinct values and the pairs arbitrary values); this is not used below.
- For a `≼`-down-set `Λ ⊆ BPar_q(α, β)` put `Z_Λ := {(ξ, η) ∈ Ω^A × Ω^B : λ(ξ, η) ∈ Λ}`.
- **Tight patterns.** A tight pattern of `λ = (λ_+, λ_−) ∈ BPar_q(α, β)` on `(A, B)` consists of a set `P` of `α − |λ_+| = β − |λ_−|` pairwise disjoint pairs `(i, l) ∈ A × B`, of blocks `B^+_1, …, B^+_{λ_{+,1}}` partitioning `A ∖ A(P)` with `|B^+_c| = (λ_+)'_c`, and of blocks `B^−_1, …, B^−_{λ_{−,1}}` partitioning `B ∖ B(P)` with `|B^−_c| = (λ_−)'_c`. Its **product** is

`Π_{(i,l) ∈ P}(x_i − z_l)^{q−1} · Π_c Δ(x_{B^+_c}) · Π_c Δ(z_{B^−_c})`,

with `Δ` the Vandermonde determinant of §5.2.
- `V_Λ ⊆ R_{A,B}` is the ideal generated by the products of all tight patterns on `(A, B)` of all elements of `Λ`. For `α = β = 0` the empty product is `1`.

**Lemma 7.2 (the roots).** *(i) `{(∅, ∅)}` is a down-set of `BPar_q(a, a)`, `V_{{(∅,∅)}} = I^{bal}_a`, and `|Z_{{(∅,∅)}}| = N_{bal}(a, q)`. (ii) `{((1), ∅)}` is a down-set of `BPar_q(a+1, a)`, `V_{{((1),∅)}} = I^{ph}_a`, and `|Z_{{((1),∅)}}| = N_{ph}(a, q)`. (iii) `N_{ph}(a, q) = N_{bal}(a + 1, q)`.*

*Proof.* (i) `λ_± ≼ ∅` forces `λ_± = ∅`. A tight pattern of `(∅, ∅)` is a perfect matching of `A` with `B`, i.e. a bijection `σ`, with product `Π_i (x_i − z_{σ(i)})^{q−1}`; and the shape is `(∅, ∅)` iff `X = Y`. (ii) In `BPar_q(a+1, a)`, `|λ_+| = |λ_−| + 1`; `λ_− ≼ ∅` forces `λ_− = ∅` and then `λ_+ ≼ (1)` with `|λ_+| = 1` forces `λ_+ = (1)`. A tight pattern has `a` pairs and one `x`-block of size `1` (with `Δ = 1`), so its product is a generator of `I^{ph}_a`, and every generator arises. The shape is `((1), ∅)` iff `Y ⊆ X`. (iii) Given `(ξ, η) ∈ Ω^{a+1} × Ω^a` with `Y ⊆ X`, append to `η` the one element of `X − Y`; given a balanced point in `Ω^{a+1} × Ω^{a+1}`, delete the last coordinate of `η`. These maps are mutually inverse. ∎

For instance, `N_{bal}(a, 3) = 3, 15, 93, 639, 4653` and `N_{bal}(2, q) = 15, 45, 91, 153, 231` for `a = 1, …, 5` and `q = 3, 5, 7, 9, 11`.

**Lemma 7.2′ (§5.5 and §5.6 for arbitrary partitions).** *Let `λ ≼ λ̃` be partitions of arbitrary sizes and lengths. Then the identities of §5.5, the chain of Lemma 5.5 for the removals and the additions, (T) of §5.6, the inequality (5.2) of §5.6 (if `ℓ(λ) < t ≤ ℓ(λ̃)`, then `S_t(λ̃) ≥ |λ| + (t − ℓ(λ))`) and the comparisons (a), (b), (c), (e2) of §5.6, recalled in the proof of Proposition 7.4, hold for `λ` and `λ̃`.*

*Proof.* The identities of §5.5 and the chain are statements about one partition. The proofs of (T), (5.2), (a), (b), (c) and (e2) in §5.6 compare the partial sums `S_t` of the two partitions term by term and use only `λ ≼ λ̃`. In §5.6 the parity of `|μ|` is used only in case (d1), and the cap `ℓ ≤ h` only in the layout of the positions and in case (e1); none of these is borrowed here. ∎

### 7.3 (P1) Fibres and (Chain)
Fix a down-set `Λ ⊆ BPar_q(α, β)` with `α ≥ 1`, and peel `w = x_{i_1}`. Let `M'` be a tail with shape `μ = (μ_+, μ_−) ∈ BPar_q(α − 1, β)`, residual values `u_1, …, u_{ℓ_+}` of `R_+` (`u_j` with multiplicity `μ_{+,j}`) and `v_1, …, v_{ℓ_−}` of `R_−`. Adding the value `u` at the index `i_1` changes the shape as follows:
- `u = v_j`: `(μ_+, μ_− − e_j)` (one box removed from row `j` of `μ_−`, re-sorted);
- `u = u_j`: `(μ_+ + e_j, μ_−)`;
- `u` any of the `q − ℓ_+ − ℓ_−` other values (the values whose multiplicities in the two coordinate sets of the tail agree, possibly `0 = 0`): `(μ_+ ⊔ 1, μ_−)`.

These are `q = |Ω|` values, and every resulting shape lies in `BPar_q(α, β)`: one value is added to the `x`-side, and a middle value exists only when `ℓ_+ + ℓ_− < q`, which gives `ℓ(μ_+ ⊔ 1) + ℓ(μ_−) ≤ q`. List them by position `p = 1, …, q`: `opt_p(μ) := (μ_+, μ_− − e_p)` for `p ≤ ℓ_−`; `(μ_+ ⊔ 1, μ_−)` for `ℓ_− < p ≤ q − ℓ_+` (the **middle** positions); `(μ_+ + e_{q+1−p}, μ_−)` for `p > q − ℓ_+`. Hence `|F(M')| = F_Λ(μ) := #{p : opt_p(μ) ∈ Λ}`, and `Z_{>i} = Z_{Λ_i}` with `Λ_i := {μ ∈ BPar_q(α − 1, β) : F_Λ(μ) > i}`.

**Lemma 7.3 (chain).** *`opt_1(μ) ≼ opt_2(μ) ≼ ⋯ ≼ opt_q(μ)`. Hence the options lying in `Λ` form an initial segment, of length `F_Λ(μ)`, and*

`F_Λ(μ) = r + (q − ℓ_+ − ℓ_−)·ι + (ℓ_+ − j_0 + 1)·[j_0 exists]`,   (7.1)

*where `r = 0` or `r` is the last row of `μ_−` of its length (the removals in `Λ` are those at positions `≤ r`), `ι := [(μ_+ ⊔ 1, μ_−) ∈ Λ]` (`ι := 0` if `ℓ_+ + ℓ_− = q`), and `j_0`, if it exists, is the first row of `μ_+` of its length such that `(μ_+ + e_j, μ_−) ∈ Λ` exactly for `j_0 ≤ j ≤ ℓ_+`. If `j_0` exists, then `r = ℓ_−`, and either `ι = 1` or `ℓ_+ + ℓ_− = q`; if `ι = 1`, then `r = ℓ_−`.*

*Proof.* By the identities of §5.5 (Lemma 7.2′), `μ_− − e_1 ≼ ⋯ ≼ μ_− − e_{ℓ_−} ≼ μ_−` and `μ_+ ≼ μ_+ ⊔ 1 ≼ μ_+ + e_{ℓ_+} ≼ ⋯ ≼ μ_+ + e_1`. Along the removals the first component is `μ_+`, and along the middle and the additions the second component is `μ_−`; so in the product order the list is weakly increasing, including the step from the last removal to the first middle or addition option. Since `Λ` is a down-set, the options in `Λ` form an initial segment. Rows of equal length give equal options, which gives the description of `r` and `j_0`. ∎

In particular, with `Φ := F_Λ(μ)`: `Φ = q − j_0 + 1` if `j_0` exists, `Φ = q − ℓ_+` if `j_0` does not exist and `ι = 1`, and `Φ = r` otherwise.

### 7.4 (P2) The layers are down-sets
**Proposition 7.4 (inequality of pairs of partitions).** *If `μ, μ̃ ∈ BPar_q(α − 1, β)` and `μ ≼ μ̃`, then `opt_p(μ) ≼ opt_p(μ̃)` for every `p`. Hence `F_Λ(μ) ≥ F_Λ(μ̃)` and every `Λ_i` is a down-set.*

*Proof.* The second sentence follows from the first as in Proposition 5.6. Write `ℓ_± := ℓ(μ_±)`, `ℓ̃_± := ℓ(μ̃_±)`. Since `|μ_+| − |μ_−| = |μ̃_+| − |μ̃_−|`, any strict inequality `|μ̃_−| > |μ_−|` gives `|μ̃_+| > |μ_+|`. We use (T) of §5.6 and the inequality (5.2) (Lemma 7.2′): if `λ ≼ λ̃` and `ℓ(λ) < t ≤ ℓ(λ̃)`, then `S_t(λ̃) ≥ |λ| + (t − ℓ(λ))`. We also use four comparisons of §5.6, valid for arbitrary partitions by Lemma 7.2′: **(a)** `λ − e_p ≼ λ̃ − e_p` for `p ≤ min(ℓ(λ), ℓ(λ̃))`; **(b)** `λ + e_j ≼ λ̃ + e_j` for `j ≤ min(ℓ(λ), ℓ(λ̃))`; **(c)** `λ ⊔ 1 ≼ λ̃ ⊔ 1`; **(e2)** `λ + e_j ≼ λ̃ ⊔ 1` for `ℓ(λ̃) < j ≤ ℓ(λ)`. Each position `p` is a removal (R), a middle position (M) or an addition (A) for `μ`, and likewise for `μ̃`, which gives nine cases; put `j := q + 1 − p` when `p` is an addition.

| case | the position `p` is | option of `μ` | option of `μ̃` | used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RR | a removal for both | `(μ_+, μ_− − e_p)` | `(μ̃_+, μ̃_− − e_p)` | `μ_+ ≼ μ̃_+`; (a) |
| AA | an addition for both | `(μ_+ + e_j, μ_−)` | `(μ̃_+ + e_j, μ̃_−)` | (b); `μ_− ≼ μ̃_−` |
| MM | middle for both | `(μ_+ ⊔ 1, μ_−)` | `(μ̃_+ ⊔ 1, μ̃_−)` | (c); `μ_− ≼ μ̃_−` |
| RM, RA | a removal for `μ` | `(μ_+, μ_− − e_p)` | middle or addition | the chain through `μ ≼ μ̃` |
| AM | an addition for `μ`, middle for `μ̃` | `(μ_+ + e_j, μ_−)` | `(μ̃_+ ⊔ 1, μ̃_−)` | (e2); `μ_− ≼ μ̃_−` |
| MA | middle for `μ`, an addition for `μ̃` | `(μ_+ ⊔ 1, μ_−)` | `(μ̃_+ + e_j, μ̃_−)` | dominance, (5.2), sizes; `μ_− ≼ μ̃_−` |
| MR | middle for `μ`, a removal for `μ̃` | `(μ_+ ⊔ 1, μ_−)` | `(μ̃_+, μ̃_− − e_p)` | (5.2) and the transfer of sizes |
| AR | an addition for `μ`, a removal for `μ̃` | `(μ_+ + e_j, μ_−)` | `(μ̃_+, μ̃_− − e_p)` | (T), (5.2) and the transfer of sizes |

The «transfer of sizes» is the one genuinely bipartite step: since `|μ_+| − |μ_−| = |μ̃_+| − |μ̃_−|`, a strict inequality on the `z`-side gives one on the `x`-side.
- **RR** (`p ≤ min(ℓ_−, ℓ̃_−)`): first components `μ_+ ≼ μ̃_+`; second components by (a).
- **AA** (`j ≤ min(ℓ_+, ℓ̃_+)`): (b), and `μ_− ≼ μ̃_−`.
- **MM**: (c), and `μ_− ≼ μ̃_−`.
- **RM**: `(μ_+, μ_− − e_p) ≼ μ ≼ μ̃ ≼ (μ̃_+ ⊔ 1, μ̃_−)`.
- **RA**: `(μ_+, μ_− − e_p) ≼ μ ≼ μ̃ ≼ (μ̃_+ + e_j, μ̃_−)`.
- **AM** (`j ≤ ℓ_+` and `ℓ̃_+ < j`): (e2), and `μ_− ≼ μ̃_−`.
- **MA** (`p` middle for `μ`, so `j > ℓ_+`; addition for `μ̃`, so `j ≤ ℓ̃_+`): we need `μ_+ ⊔ 1 ≼ μ̃_+ + e_j`. For `t ≤ ℓ_+`, `S_t(μ_+ ⊔ 1) = S_t(μ_+) ≤ S_t(μ̃_+) ≤ S_t(μ̃_+ + e_j)`. For `ℓ_+ < t ≤ ℓ̃_+`, `S_t(μ̃_+) ≥ |μ_+| + 1 = S_t(μ_+ ⊔ 1)` by (5.2). For `t > ℓ̃_+ ≥ j ≥ r̲_j(μ̃_+)`, `S_t(μ̃_+ + e_j) = |μ̃_+| + 1 ≥ |μ_+| + 1`. The second components are `μ_− ≼ μ̃_−`.
- **MR** (`p` middle for `μ`, so `p > ℓ_−`; removal for `μ̃`, so `p ≤ ℓ̃_−`): by (5.2) at `t = ℓ̃_−`, `|μ̃_−| ≥ |μ_−| + 1`, hence `|μ̃_+| ≥ |μ_+| + 1`. *First components:* `μ_+ ⊔ 1 ≼ μ̃_+`: for `t ≤ ℓ_+` this is dominance; for `t > ℓ_+` we need `S_t(μ̃_+) ≥ |μ_+| + 1`, which holds by (5.2) if `t < ℓ̃_+` and by `|μ̃_+| ≥ |μ_+| + 1` if `t ≥ ℓ̃_+`. *Second components:* `μ_− ≼ μ̃_− − e_p`: for `t < r̄_p(μ̃_−)` this is dominance; for `t ≥ r̄_p(μ̃_−) ≥ p > ℓ_−`, `S_t(μ_−) = |μ_−|`, while the rows `ℓ_− + 1, …, p` of `μ̃_−` are non-empty, so `S_t(μ̃_−) ≥ S_{ℓ_−}(μ̃_−) + 1 ≥ |μ_−| + 1`.
- **AR** (`p` addition for `μ`, so `j ≤ ℓ_+`; removal for `μ̃`, so `p ≤ ℓ̃_−`): since `ℓ_+ + ℓ_− ≤ q` and `ℓ̃_+ + ℓ̃_− ≤ q`, we have `ℓ_− ≤ q − ℓ_+ < p` and `ℓ̃_+ ≤ q − p = j − 1`. *Second components:* exactly as in MR, `μ_− ≼ μ̃_− − e_p` and `|μ̃_−| ≥ |μ_−| + 1`, so `|μ̃_+| ≥ |μ_+| + 1`. *First components:* we need `S_t(μ_+) + [t ≥ r̲_j(μ_+)] ≤ S_t(μ̃_+)`. This is dominance for `t < r̲_j(μ_+)`, and it follows from `|μ̃_+| ≥ |μ_+| + 1` for `t ≥ ℓ̃_+`. It remains to show `S_t(μ_+) < S_t(μ̃_+)` for `r̲_j(μ_+) ≤ t < ℓ̃_+`. Suppose `t` is tight. The rows `r̲_j(μ_+), …, j` of `μ_+` have the same length `c := μ_{+,j} ≥ 1`, and `t < t + 1 ≤ ℓ̃_+ ≤ j − 1`, so `μ_{+,t} = μ_{+,t+1} = c`. By (T), `c = μ_{+,t} ≥ μ̃_{+,t} ≥ μ̃_{+,t+1} ≥ μ_{+,t+1} = c`, so every row of `μ̃_+` after the `t`-th has length `≤ c`. As `μ̃_+` has only `ℓ̃_+ ≤ j − 1` rows, `S_j(μ̃_+) ≤ S_t(μ_+) + (j − 1 − t)c < S_t(μ_+) + (j − t)c = S_j(μ_+)`, contradicting `μ_+ ≼ μ̃_+`.

For each of `μ` and `μ̃` the three types partition `{1, …, q}`, so the nine cases cover every position. ∎

### 7.5 (P3) Every tight pattern of a layer lies in the right slice
**Proposition 7.5.** *For every `i ∈ {0, …, q − 1}`, `V_{Λ_i} ⊆ W_{q−1−i}(V_Λ)`, where `V_{Λ_i} ⊆ R_{A ∖ {i_1}, B}`.*

*Proof.* It suffices to treat a generator `G`: the product of a tight pattern `(P, (B^+_c), (B^−_c))` of some `μ ∈ Λ_i`, so that `Φ := F_Λ(μ) ≥ i + 1`. By Lemma 7.1(ii) it is enough to find `f ∈ V_Λ` with `deg_w f ≤ q − Φ` and `[w^{q−Φ}]f = ±κ·G` for a non-zero constant `κ`; then `G ∈ W_{q−Φ}(V_Λ) ⊆ W_{q−1−i}(V_Λ)`. By (7.1) exactly one of the following holds.
- **(α)** `j_0` exists and `λ := (μ_+ + e_{j_0}, μ_−) ∈ Λ`, `Φ = q − j_0 + 1`. The column `c^* := μ_{+,j_0} + 1` of `μ_+` has height `j_0 − 1`, because `j_0` is the first row of its length; let `S := B^+_{c^*}` (if `j_0 = 1`, `c^*` is a new column and `S = ∅`). Putting `i_1` into `S` gives a tight pattern of `λ`: column `c^*` of `λ_+` has height `j_0`, and the number of pairs `α − |λ_+| = (α − 1) − |μ_+|` is unchanged. Its product lies in `V_Λ`; by the factorisation `Δ(x_{S ∪ i_1}) = ±Π_{s ∈ S}(w − x_s)·Δ(x_S)` it has `w`-degree `|S| = j_0 − 1 = q − Φ ≤ q − 1` (so `w^{|S|} ≠ 0` in the box) and top coefficient `±G`.
- **(β)** `j_0` does not exist, `ι = 1`, `λ := (μ_+ ⊔ 1, μ_−) ∈ Λ`, `Φ = q − ℓ_+`. Put `i_1` into `B^+_1`, of height `ℓ_+` (empty if `μ_+ = ∅`): this is a tight pattern of `λ`, whose product has `w`-degree `ℓ_+ = q − Φ ≤ q − 1` (a middle value exists, so `ℓ_+ < q`) and top coefficient `±G`.
- **(γ)** `j_0` does not exist, `ι = 0`, and `Φ = r ≥ 1`, `λ := (μ_+, μ_− − e_r) ∈ Λ`. The column `c_0 := μ_{−,r}` of `μ_−` has height `r`, because `r` is the last row of its length; let `S := B^−_{c_0}` and write `G = Δ(z_S)·G'`. For `b ∈ S`, the pairs `P ∪ {(i_1, b)}`, the blocks `B^+_c`, the blocks `B^−_c` (`c ≠ c_0`) and `S ∖ {b}` form a tight pattern of `λ`: column `c_0` of `λ_−` has height `r − 1`, and the number of pairs grows by one, as `α − |λ_+| = ((α − 1) − |μ_+|) + 1`. So, with the signs `ε_b` of Lemma 5.7(ii),

`f := Σ_{b ∈ S} ε_b Δ(z_{S∖b})·(w − z_b)^{q−1}·G' ∈ V_Λ`.

Expanding, `[w^{q−1−t}] f = C(q−1, t)(−1)^t·(Σ_{b∈S} ε_b Δ(z_{S∖b}) z_b^t)·G'`, which is `0` for `t ≤ r − 2` and `±C(q−1, r−1)·G` for `t = r − 1`, by Lemma 5.7(ii) (an identity over `Z`, valid in `R_{A ∖ {i_1}, B}`). So `deg_w f ≤ q − r = q − Φ`, and the top coefficient is `±C(q−1, r−1)·G` with `C(q−1, r−1) ≠ 0` in `F` (here `r ≤ ℓ_− ≤ q`). ∎

The three constructions land exactly in the slice `q − Φ`, and in general the slice cannot be lowered: the statement with `W_{q−2−i}` in place of `W_{q−1−i}` fails at the cells of §12.4, for some generators `G`.

### 7.6 The induction
**Theorem 7.6 (bipartite down-set theorem).** *For all `α, β ≥ 0` and every `≼`-down-set `Λ ⊆ BPar_q(α, β)`, `dim_F V_Λ ≥ |Z_Λ|`.*

*Proof.* Induction on `α + β`. **Base:** `BPar_q(0, 0) = {(∅, ∅)}`; for `Λ = ∅` both sides are `0`, and for `Λ = {(∅, ∅)}`, `V_Λ = F` and `Z_Λ` is one point. **Symmetry:** the isomorphism `R_{A,B} ≅ R_{B,A}` that exchanges the names `x` and `z` carries tight-pattern products of `(λ_+, λ_−)` on `(A, B)` to tight-pattern products of `(λ_−, λ_+)` on `(B, A)` (as `(x − z)^{q−1} = (z − x)^{q−1}`), and `(ξ, η) ↦ (η, ξ)` exchanges `λ_+` and `λ_−`. A relabelling of `A` or `B` changes each product by a sign and preserves `|Z_Λ|`. So we may assume `α ≥ 1` and peel any `x`-variable. **Step:** by Lemma 7.1(iii), Proposition 7.5, the induction hypothesis (each `Λ_i` is a down-set of `BPar_q(α − 1, β)` by Proposition 7.4), §7.3 and Lemma 7.1(iv),

`dim V_Λ = Σ_{i=0}^{q−1} dim W_{q−1−i}(V_Λ) ≥ Σ_{i=0}^{q−1} dim V_{Λ_i} ≥ Σ_{i=0}^{q−1} |Z_{Λ_i}| = Σ_{i=0}^{q−1} |Z_{>i}| = |Z_Λ|`. ∎

*Proof of Theorem C.* Lemma 7.2 and Theorem 7.6. ∎

### 7.7 Consequences
**Corollary 7.7 (Main Theorem′ and the Main Theorem).** *Main Theorem′ holds, and Conjecture 1.2 of [DS] holds for every odd `m ≥ 3` and every even dimension.*

*Proof.* Let `k ≥ 1`. For every prime `p | m`, `dim_{F_p} F_p[G]/(ψ̄_J : J ∈ 𝒥) = m^{n+1} − |Γ_𝒥|`: for `m = p^v` by Corollary 5.4 and Proposition 2.5; otherwise by Proposition 6.9, whose hypothesis is Theorem C over `F = F̄_p`, `q = p^v`. For `p ∤ m` the same equality is Theorem 0(b) with `K_p = F̄_p`. The rank of `R/(ψ_J : J ∈ 𝒥)` is `m^{n+1} − |Γ_𝒥|` (Theorem 0(b) with `p = 0`), and `|Γ_𝒥| = Q_k(m)` (Lemma 2.2, valid for odd `m`); so `R/(ψ_J : J ∈ 𝒥)` has no torsion, which is Main Theorem′. No step of this uses topology. With Theorem 0(a) (Proposition 2.1) it gives the Main Theorem; for `k = 0` the Main Theorem is trivial. ∎

**Corollary 7.8 (equality over `F̄_p`).** *Let `q = p^v` with `p` odd. Then `dim_{F̄_p} I^{bal}_a = N_{bal}(a, q)` and `dim_{F̄_p} I^{ph}_a = N_{ph}(a, q)` for every `a ≥ 0`.*

*Proof.* For `a = 0`, `I^{bal}_0 = F` and `I^{ph}_0 = F[x_1]/(x_1^q)` have dimensions `1 = N_{bal}(0, q)` and `q = N_{ph}(0, q)`. Let `a ≥ 1`, choose an odd prime `r ≠ p` and put `m := qr` and `n := 2a`, so that `k = a ≥ 1` and (6.1) applies. Colour `a` coordinates by a primitive `r`-th root `ζ`, `a` coordinates by `ζ^{−1}`, and one coordinate `s` by `1`; then `c_0 = 1`, the block `𝒞_1 = {0, s}` contributes `dim I_{c,1} = q − 1 = N_1(c)`, and the pair block contributes `dim I^{bal}_a`. If `dim I^{bal}_a > N_{bal}(a, q)`, this colouring has `dim π_c(I) > N_1(c)·N_{bal}(a, q)`, while every other compatible colouring has `dim π_c(I) ≥ N_1(c)·Π_ζ N_ζ(c)` (Proposition 6.5, Lemmas 6.6 and 6.7, and Theorem C for every `a ≤ n`). Summing, `dim_F I > |Γ_𝒥|` by Lemma 6.8, contradicting (6.1). For `I^{ph}_a`, take `n := 2a`, colour `a + 1` coordinates by `ζ` and `a` coordinates by `ζ^{−1}`; then `c_0 = ζ^{−1}`, `𝒞_1 = ∅`, and the only block is `I^{ph}_a`. ∎

*Remarks 7.9.* (1) Corollary 7.8 is an algebraic statement with an algebraic proof: the upper bound (6.1) is Theorem 0(b), a Chinese-remainder count. The proof is global, through the sum over the colourings of `F[G]`; we do not know a direct proof of `≤` on one block: the evaluation argument of Theorem 5.9 fails, because `(x − z)^{q−1}` vanishes at `x = z` rather than off it.
(2) In characteristic `0` the inequalities of Theorem C are strict in general: `dim_Q ((x − z)^2) = 4 > 3 = N_{bal}(1, 3)` in `Q[x, z]/(x^3, z^3)`, and `dim_Q I^{bal}_2 = 24 > 15` at `q = 3` (§12). So, unlike Theorem 5.9′, in these cells (where Corollary 7.8 gives `dim_{F_3} I^{bal}_a = N_{bal}(a, 3) < dim_Q I^{bal}_a`) the quotient of the integral box ring by the bipartite ideal has `3`-torsion: the bipartite ideals are genuinely of characteristic `p`.
(3) By Lemma 7.2(iii) the phantom and balanced counts are related. On the ideal side, if `σ ∈ S_{a+1}` and `i_0 := σ^{−1}(a+1)`, the generator `Π_i(x_i − z_{σ(i)})^{q−1}` of `I^{bal}_{a+1}` has `z_{a+1}`-degree `q − 1`, with top coefficient the generator of `I^{ph}_a` indexed by `(i_0, σ|)`, so the top slice `W_{q−1}(I^{bal}_{a+1})` (peeling `z_{a+1}`, that is, Lemma 7.1 with the names `x` and `z` exchanged, as in the symmetry step of Theorem 7.6) contains `I^{ph}_a`. With Corollary 7.8, `W_j(I^{bal}_{a+1}) = 0` for `j < q − 1`, and `f ↦ [z_{a+1}^{q−1}] f` is an isomorphism of graded vector spaces `I^{bal}_{a+1} → I^{ph}_a` of degree `−(q−1)` over `F̄_p`.

## 8. A different count: Theorem A

For a finite set `L` of variables over a field `F` and an odd `q ≥ 1`, put `I_q(L) := (e_j(L) : j odd) + (x^q : x ∈ L)` and

`N_q(n) := n!·[y^n] e^y I_0(2y)^{(q−1)/2}`.

**Theorem A.** *Let `F` be a field with `char F ≠ 2`, let `q ≥ 1` be odd, and let `|L| = n`. Then `dim_F F[L]/I_q(L) = N_q(n)`.*

The proof is in Appendix A. The lower bound degenerates the ideal of an explicit finite set of points. The upper bound is an induction on `n` over a family of ideals `K_μ(L)` indexed by partitions `μ`; the ideals are built from the coefficients of one generating function, and each inductive step shifts one index in that function. The proof uses the characteristic only through `2 ∈ F^×`, and it uses no relation between `q` and `char F`.

**Corollary 8.1.** *Let `p` be an odd prime and `q = p^v`. For every `k ≥ 0`, `A_k(q) := dim F_p[x_0, …, x_{2k+1}]/(E + (x_0^q, …, x_{2k+1}^q)) = P_k(q)`, where `P_k(q)` is the number of points of `F_q^{2k+2}` at which every odd elementary symmetric polynomial vanishes, i.e. the number of `F_q`-points of `⋃_{J∈𝒥} V(I_J)`.*

**Corollary 8.2.** *For every odd `q` and every `n`, the finitely generated abelian group `M := Z[x_1, …, x_n]/(e_j : j odd; x_i^q)` has rank `N_q(n)`, and its torsion subgroup is a `2`-group.*

*Remarks 8.3.* (1) In characteristic `2` the statement fails: at `n = 4` the dimensions are `21, 65, 133, 225` for `q = 3, 5, 7, 9`, against `N_q(4) = 19, 61, 127, 217`. (2) For `q = 3` and all `k`, Corollary 8.1 was known earlier through Steinberg modules and tilting theory [Jan, II.4.16], [Mat, Theorem 1(1)], [DCP, Theorems 5.6–5.7]; for `k ≤ 4` and all `q = 3^v` it was known through case-by-case arguments in earlier notes of this project. Theorem A contains all of these, in every characteristic `≠ 2` and for every odd `q`. **In characteristic `0`, however, Theorem A is not new: it follows from a theorem of Kostant (Remark 8.4).** (3) **Theorem A is not used anywhere in the proof of the Main Theorem, and it does not imply it** (§9).

**Remark 8.4 (Kostant's theorem, and what is new in Theorem A).** Let `q = 2m + 1 ≥ 3`.

(1) *A dictionary.* Let `F` be a field of characteristic `0`, and let `V := F[x]/(x^q)` with the bilinear form `⟨x^a, x^b⟩ := (−1)^a` if `a + b = q − 1`, and `0` otherwise. The form is nondegenerate, and it is symmetric because `a + b` is even. Multiplication by `x`, which we call `e`, satisfies `⟨e u, v⟩ = −⟨u, e v⟩`. So `e` lies in the orthogonal Lie algebra `𝔤 := so(V) ≅ so_{2m+1}`, and it is nilpotent with a single Jordan block: a principal (regular) nilpotent element. An endomorphism of `V` that commutes with `e` is a polynomial in `e`, and it is skew exactly when the polynomial is odd; so the centralizer `𝔤^e` has basis `e, e^3, …, e^{2m−1}`. The ring `F[L]/(x^q : x ∈ L)` is `V^{⊗n}`, and `e^j ∈ 𝔤` acts on it as multiplication by the power sum `p_j(L)`. In characteristic `0` the ideals `(e_j(L) : j odd)` and `(p_j(L) : j odd)` coincide, by Newton's identities: every monomial of `p_j` in the `e`'s has weight `j`, so for odd `j` it contains an `e` of odd index; and `j·e_j = Σ_{i=1}^{j} (−1)^{i−1} e_{j−i} p_i`, where for odd `j` every term has `i` odd or `j − i` odd. Finally `p_j(L) ∈ (x^q : x ∈ L)` for `j ≥ q`. Hence

`F[L]/I_q(L) = V^{⊗n} / 𝔤^e·V^{⊗n}`,

the coinvariants of the centralizer of a principal nilpotent element on the `n`-th tensor power of the vector representation of `so_{2m+1}`. Since `V^{⊗n}` is self-dual, this space has the dimension of the invariants `(V^{⊗n})^{𝔤^e}`.

(2) *Kostant's theorem.* Let `G` be a connected reductive group over `C` and `W` a finite-dimensional rational `G`-module whose weights lie in the root lattice. Then `x ↦ dim W^{𝔤^x}` is constant on the regular elements of `𝔤` [Kos]; we quote the statement from [Gin, Proposition 4.2]. At a regular semisimple `x` the centralizer `𝔤^x` is a Cartan subalgebra, and `W^{𝔤^x}` is the zero weight space of `W`. The weights of `V` are `0, ±ε_1, …, ±ε_m`, which span the root lattice of `so_{2m+1}`. So `dim (V^{⊗n})^{𝔤^e}` is the dimension of the zero weight space of `V^{⊗n}`: the number of sequences of `n` weights of `V` with sum `0`, that is, of closed walks of length `n` on `Z^m` with steps `0, ±ε_i`, which is `N_q(n)`. The dimension of `F[L]/I_q(L)` does not depend on the field of characteristic `0`. **So in characteristic `0`, Theorem A follows from Kostant's theorem and (1).** We have not found the dictionary (1) in the literature, and the earlier versions of this paper did not cite Kostant; the count in characteristic `0` should nevertheless be credited to his theorem.

(3) *What Theorem A adds.* The argument of (1)–(2) is not available in characteristic `p`: Newton's identities divide by odd integers, and Kostant's theorem is a statement in characteristic `0`. Theorem A gives the same count in every characteristic `≠ 2`, including `p ≤ q`, and with it Corollary 8.2. A group-theoretic description survives over every field in which `2` is invertible. The transformation `g_t := (1 + te)(1 − te)^{−1}` preserves the form and commutes with `e`, and it acts on `V^{⊗n}` as multiplication by `E(t)/E(−t)`, where `E(t) := Π_{x∈L}(1 + tx) = Σ_j e_j(L) t^j`. Since `E(t) − E(−t) = 2 Σ_{j odd} e_j(L) t^j` and `E(−t)` is an invertible power series in `t`, the coefficients of `E(t)/E(−t) − 1` generate the ideal `(e_j(L) : j odd)`. So `F[L]/I_q(L)` is the space of coinvariants of the family `{g_t}` in `V^{⊗n}`, and Theorem A says that its dimension is that of the zero weight space, in every characteristic `≠ 2`. We do not know a version of Kostant's theorem in positive characteristic that gives this. For `q = 3` it is the statement recalled in Remark 8.3(2).

(4) *Computed, not claimed.* (a) In the `27` cells `(q, n)` that we computed (`3 ≤ q ≤ 13`, `n ≤ 9`), the Hilbert series of `F[L]/I_q(L)` is `Σ_λ c_λ·t^{mn} m^0_λ(t^{−1})`, where `λ` runs over the irreducible constituents `V_λ` of `V^{⊗n}`, `c_λ` is the multiplicity, and `m^0_λ` is Lusztig's `t`-analogue of the zero weight multiplicity [Lus]; in `56` cases the same holds for the graded character of the symmetric group (pairs of a cell with `q ∈ {3, 5}`, `n ≤ 6` and a cycle type), with the multiplicity spaces in place of the `c_λ`. This is what the theory of generalized exponents predicts in characteristic `0`; we have not read the sources, and we record it only as a computation (§12.5). (b) For even `q = 2m` the form of (1) is alternating, `e` is a principal nilpotent of `sp_{2m}`, and for even `n` Kostant's theorem gives, in characteristic `0`, the dimension `n!·[y^n] I_0(2y)^m`, the number of closed walks of length `n` on `Z^m` with steps `±ε_i`. In `8` cells `(q, n)` with `q ∈ {2, 4, 6, 8}`, of both parities of `n`, the dimension in characteristics `3`, `5` and `7` equals the dimension in characteristic `0`, and for odd `n` it is the number of such walks from `0` to `ε_1`; in characteristic `2` it is larger in all `8` cells. We do not prove this (§13).

## 9. Why the full family is needed

### 9.1 Two counts
Let `p` be an odd prime, `q = p^v`, `S := F_p[x_0, …, x_{2k+1}]`, `N = 2k+2` and `I_J := (x_a + x_b : {a,b} ∈ J)`. The ideal `E` vanishes on `⋃_J V(I_J)`: a point lies there iff its multiset of coordinates is closed under negation iff `Π_i(1 + x_i t)` is even in `t` (unique factorisation in `F̄_p[t]`). By Proposition 2.6, (S) says

`b_k(q−1) := dim S/⋂_{J∈𝒥}(I_J + (x_i^{q−1})) = Q_k(q)`,

an **intersection** at the **even** box `q − 1`, counting the points of the union of the matching spaces on the **zero-free** grid `T^N`. Corollary 8.1 says `dim S/(E + (x_i^q)) = P_k(q)`, a **sum** at the **odd** box `q`, counting points on the grid **with** `0`.

**Proposition 9.1 (the sandwich).** *`b_k(q−1) ≤ Q_k(q) ≤ dim S/(E + (x_i^{q−1}))`.*

*Proof.* Dimensions do not change under extension of scalars, so we may work over `F_q`. Let `Γ ⊆ T^N` be the set of zero-free points of `⋃_J V(I_J)`; `|Γ| = Q_k(q)` by Lemma 2.2, and `S/I(Γ)` has dimension `|Γ|`. We use Lemma 5.8′ (with `N` variables). The ideal `I'' := E + (x_i^{q−1} − 1)` is contained in `I(Γ)`, and `gr(I'') ⊇ E + (x_i^{q−1})` because `E` is homogeneous; so `dim S/(E + (x_i^{q−1})) ≥ dim S/gr(I'') = dim S/I'' ≥ |Γ|`, the right-hand inequality. For each `J`, the ideal of the finite set `V(I_J) ∩ T^N` is `I_J + (x_i^{q−1} − 1)`: modulo `I_J` one free variable per pair remains, and `x^{q−1} − 1` is separable with roots exactly `T`. Its `gr` contains `I_J + (x_i^{q−1})`, and is equal to it, since both quotients have dimension `(q−1)^{k+1}`. Finally `I(Γ) = ⋂_J I(V(I_J) ∩ T^N)`, and `gr` of an intersection is contained in the intersection of the `gr`'s, so `gr(I(Γ)) ⊆ ⋂_J (I_J + (x_i^{q−1}))`, which gives the left-hand inequality. ∎

So the Main Theorem is the **left** equality. It is not a consequence of Corollary 8.1, which is an equality of the right-hand kind at a different box.

### 9.2 A counterexample for subfamilies
Proposition 2.1 and Proposition 2.5 hold for every non-empty `K ⊆ 𝒥`. So, for a subfamily, `L_K(X)` is primitive iff `dim(D_J : J ∈ K)C = |Γ_K|`.

**Fact 9.2 (computed; not used in any proof).** *Let `q = 9`, `k = 2`, and let `K := 𝒥 ∖ {[[0,1],[2,3],[4,5]], [[0,2],[1,5],[3,4]]}` (13 of the 15 matchings). Then `dim(D_J : J ∈ K)C = 4730 < 4736 = |Γ_K|`. So `H_4(X)/L_K(X)` has `3`-torsion for the Fermat fourfold of degree `9`. For the same `K`, the sum-type count holds: with `E_K := ⋂_{J∈K} I_J`, `dim S/(E_K + (x_i^9)) = 7089`, which is the number of `F_9`-points of `⋃_{J∈K} V(I_J)`.*

The value `4730` was obtained by four independent routes (§12): the literal form of [DS] in the ring `F_3[G]` (`dim F_3[G]/(ψ̄_J : J ∈ K) = 54 319 = 9^5 − 4730`, against `9^5 − |Γ_K| = 54 313`), the `y`-form of §2, the intersection form of Proposition 2.6, and an engine written separately (a kernel basis `D·y_a^i` per pair, exact rank modulo `3` degree by degree). `|Γ_K| = 4736` was obtained by direct enumeration.

At `q = 3`, `k = 2`, the analogue of (S) fails for `800` of the `32 767` non-empty subfamilies (worst deficit `2`, e.g. `18 < 20`), while every subfamily satisfies `≤`. So `3`-torsion for subfamilies occurs already for the cubic fourfold.

**Consequences.**
- Any argument that proves the analogue of (S) for every subfamily proves a false statement. A proof of the Main Theorem must use a property of the full family `𝒥`; in §5 this property enters at the root (Lemma 5.2), and in §6–§7 through the product structure of the compatible matchings (Proposition 6.5) and the roots of Lemma 7.2.
- `A = P` cannot imply the Main Theorem through any argument that is valid for subfamilies.
- This is the natural first guess, and it is the error described in §1.8.

## 10. The integral Hodge conjecture

In this section `k ≥ 1`, `n = 2k`, `m ≥ 3` is odd, and `X ⊂ P^{n+1}` is the Fermat variety of degree `m`.

### 10.1 Classical facts
- **(H1) Integral cohomology.** `H^*(X; Z)` is torsion free and vanishes in odd degrees. By the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem [Mil, §7], `H^{2j}(X; Z) = Z·h^j` for `2j < n`, where `h` is the hyperplane class, and `H_{2l}(X; Z) → H_{2l}(P^{n+1}; Z) ≅ Z` is an isomorphism for `2l < n`.
- **(H2) Hodge classes.** Through Poincaré duality we call a class in `H_n(X; Z)` a Hodge class if its dual lies in `H^{k,k}(X)`, and write `Hdg(X) ⊆ H_n(X; Z)` for the group of Hodge classes. It is the set of integral classes on which every class of `F^{k+1}H^n(X, C)` integrates to zero, so it is a primitive subgroup. The class of an algebraic subvariety of dimension `k` is a Hodge class, so `L(X) ⊆ Hdg(X)`. The class `P_X` of Appendix B, Step 3 (the dual of `h^k`) is a Hodge class, and `H^n(X; Q) = H^n_{prim}(X; Q) ⊕ Q·h^k`.
- **(H3) Characters [SK], [Shi79], [Ran, Proposition 1.7].** Let `𝔄` be the set of `α = (a_0, …, a_{n+1}) ∈ (Z/m ∖ {0})^{n+2}` with `a_0 + ⋯ + a_{n+1} = 0`, and for `α ∈ 𝔄` put `|α| := Σ_i ⟨a_i⟩/m`, where `⟨a⟩ ∈ {1, …, m−1}` is the representative of `a`. Under the action of `μ_m^{n+2}` on the coordinates, `H^n_{prim}(X; C) = ⊕_{α∈𝔄} V(α)` with every `V(α)` of dimension one, and `(H^{k,k}(X) ∩ H^n_{prim}(X; Q)) ⊗ C = ⊕_{α∈𝔅} V(α)`, where `𝔅 := {α ∈ 𝔄 : |tα| = k + 1 for every t ∈ (Z/m)^×}` is the set of **Hodge characters**. So `rank Hdg(X) = |𝔅| + 1`. An element `α ∈ 𝔄` is of **pair type** if the multiset `{a_0, …, a_{n+1}}` splits into pairs `{a, −a}`; we write `𝔇 ⊆ 𝔄` for the set of these.
- **(H4) Prime degree [Ran, Proposition 1.8(i)], based on [KO].** If `m` is prime, every Hodge character is of pair type. We include a short proof, so that (H4) does not depend on [Ran] or [KO].
- **(H5) Aoki's theorem [Aok, Theorem A, p. 24].** *`𝔅 = 𝔇` if and only if `m` is a prime or `4`, or every prime divisor of `m` is greater than `n + 2`.* (Aoki's sets `𝔄^n_m`, `𝔅^n_m`, `𝔇^n_m` [Aok, §0] are the sets `𝔄`, `𝔅`, `𝔇` above, with `a_i ∈ {1, …, m−1}` and `Σ a_i ≡ 0 (mod m)`; the proof of his Theorem A is in [Aok, §7], pp. 41–43, and the erratum [Aok] concerns only his Theorem B.) For odd `m` and even `n`, «greater than `n + 2`» means «at least `2k + 3`».

*Proof of (H4).* Let `m = p` and `α ∈ 𝔅`, and for `c ∈ F_p^×` let `N(c)` be the number of `i` with `a_i = c`. Then `|tα| = Σ_c N(c)⟨tc⟩/p = k + 1` for every `t ∈ F_p^×`. Let `χ` be an odd character of `F_p^×`, that is, `χ(−1) = −1`. Multiply by `χ(t)` and sum over `t`. The constant contributes `0`, and the substitution `u = tc` gives `B_{1,χ} · Σ_c N(c)χ̄(c) = 0`, where `B_{1,χ} = (1/p) Σ_{u=1}^{p−1} χ(u)u`. An odd character is non-principal, hence primitive with conductor `p` since `p` is prime; so `B_{1,χ} ≠ 0`, by the classical formula `L(1, χ̄) = πi τ(χ̄) B_{1,χ}/p` together with `L(1, χ̄) ≠ 0` [Was, Theorem 4.9]. Hence `Σ_c N(c)χ̄(c) = 0` for every odd `χ`. The function `f(c) = N(c) − N(−c)` is odd, so `Σ_c f(c)χ̄(c)` equals `2Σ_c N(c)χ̄(c) = 0` for odd `χ`, and vanishes trivially for even `χ`. By Fourier inversion on `F_p^×`, `f = 0`. Since `p` is odd, `c ≠ −c`, and the entries split into pairs `{c, −c}`. ∎ (We also checked numerically that `B_{1,χ} ≠ 0` for all `2 090` odd characters of the odd primes `p < 200`.)

### 10.2 Pair-type characters
**Lemma 10.1.** *(i) Every pair-type `α ∈ 𝔄` is a Hodge character: `𝔇 ⊆ 𝔅`. (ii) `|𝔇| = Q_k(m)`.*

*Proof.* (i) For `t ∈ (Z/m)^×` and `a ≠ 0`, `ta ≠ 0` and `⟨ta⟩ + ⟨−ta⟩ = m`. Summing over the `k + 1` pairs, `|tα| = k + 1`. (ii) Identify `Z/m` with `μ_m` by `a ↦ e^{2πia/m}`. The pair-type elements of `𝔄` become the `N`-tuples in `μ_m ∖ {1}` whose multiset is closed under inversion; the condition `Σ a_i = 0` is then automatic. By Lemma 2.2, whose proof uses only that `m` is odd, there are `Q_k(m)` of them. ∎

### 10.3 Proof of Corollary H
By (H3), (H2), Theorem 0(c) and Lemmas 2.2 and 10.1, `rank Hdg(X) = |𝔅| + 1` and `rank L(X) = |Γ_𝒥| + 1 = |𝔇| + 1`, with `L(X) ⊆ Hdg(X)` and `𝔇 ⊆ 𝔅`.

*(ii).* `Hdg(X)/L(X)` is a subgroup of `H_n(X; Z)/L(X)`, which is finitely generated and torsion free by the Main Theorem; so `Hdg(X)/L(X)` is free, of rank `|𝔅| − |𝔇| = |𝔅| − Q_k(m)`. It is zero iff `𝔅 = 𝔇`, which by (H5) happens, for odd `m`, iff `m` is prime or every prime factor of `m` is at least `2k + 3`. (For prime `m`, (H4) gives `𝔅 = 𝔇` without appeal to [Aok].)

*(i).* Under the hypothesis of (i), `Hdg(X) = L(X)` by (ii). (Only the primitivity of `L(X)` is used here; that `Hdg(X)` is primitive is not needed.) For the other degrees, by (H1) (the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem) and Poincaré duality: odd degrees vanish; for `2j < n`, `H^{2j}(X; Z)` is generated by `h^j`, the class of the intersection of `X` with `j` general hyperplanes; for `2j > n`, `H^{2j}(X; Z) ≅ H_{2l}(X; Z) ≅ Z` with `l = n − j < k`, and it is generated by the class of any linear subspace `P^l ⊆ X`, whose image in `H_{2l}(P^{n+1}; Z)` is a generator. Such subspaces exist, since `X` contains the `k`-spaces `L_{J,β}`. So every integral Hodge class of `X`, in every degree, is the class of an algebraic cycle with integer coefficients. ∎

**Remark 10.2.** (1) Under the hypothesis of (i), Corollary H says more than the integral Hodge conjecture for `X`: the integral Hodge classes of middle degree are generated by the `(2k+1)!!·m^{k+1}` standard `k`-spaces alone. (2) In the same degrees, the elementary divisors and the discriminant of the lattice of integral Hodge classes of middle degree, computed in [AMV] for small `(n, m)`, are those of the lattice generated by the standard spaces. For `k = 1` and `m = p ≥ 5` prime, closed forms are given by the author's *Watermark* and *Double Ladder* theorems [WDL], which are cited and not proved here (both are proved for every such prime and verified in Lean 4 in [WDL]; they have not been refereed): `|disc| = p^{3(p−3)^2}`, and the discriminant group is `(Z/p)^{3p^2−24p+59} × (Z/p^2)^{3p−16}` for `p ≥ 7` and `(Z/5)^{10} × Z/25` for `p = 5`. They agree with the surface rows `m = 5, 7, 11, 13` of [AMV, Table 1]. For `k ≥ 2` this is open (§13).

**Lemma 10.4 (`m = 9`).** *For `m = 9` and every `k ≥ 1`, `|𝔅| = C(2k+2, k+1)^3`. Hence `Hdg(X)/L(X)` is free of rank `C(2k+2, k+1)^3 − Q_k(9)` (`48`, `2 880`, `152 880`, `8 064 000` for `k = 1, 2, 3, 4`).*

*Proof.* Since `(Z/9)^× = {±1, ±2, ±4}` and `⟨−x⟩ = 9 − ⟨x⟩`, the condition `|tα| = k + 1` for `t` implies it for `−t`, and `α ∈ 𝔄` is a Hodge character iff `Σ_i δ(a_i) = 0`, where `δ(a) := (2⟨a⟩ − 9, 2⟨2a⟩ − 9, 2⟨4a⟩ − 9) ∈ Z^3` (note `Σ_i 9 = 9N = 18(k + 1)`). Let `s(a) ∈ {±1}^3` be the vector of signs of `δ(a)`. Then `δ(1) = (−7, −5, −1)`, `δ(2) = (−5, −1, 7)`, `δ(4) = (−1, 7, 5)`, `δ(3) = (−3, 3, −3)`, and `δ(−a) = −δ(a)`; so `a ↦ s(a)` is a bijection from `Z/9 ∖ {0}` onto `{±1}^3`, and `δ(a) = M·s(a)` for the matrix `M` with rows `(4, 2, 1)`, `(−1, 4, 2)`, `(−2, −1, 4)` (check on `a = 1, 2, 4, 3`, then use `δ(−a) = −δ(a)`). As `det M = 81 ≠ 0`, `Σ_i δ(a_i) = 0` iff `Σ_i s(a_i) = 0` in `Z^3`, i.e. iff each of the three coordinates of the sign vectors is balanced over the `N` entries. The condition `Σ a_i = 0` follows from `Σ⟨a_i⟩ = 9(k + 1)`. So `|𝔅|` is the number of `N`-tuples of sign vectors with three balanced coordinates, `C(N, N/2)^3`. ∎

(The direct counts `216`, `8 000`, `343 000` at `k = 1, 2, 3` suggested the lemma; the direct count at `k = 4`, `16 003 008 = 252^3`, agrees with it, §12.)

## 11. Partial Fermat varieties

In this section `m ≥ 3` is odd and `0 ≤ s ≤ d`. We prove Corollary W of §1.4. For `d = 0` both statements are trivial (`X` and `W_0` are sets of `m` points), so let `d ≥ 1`.

*Proof of Corollary W(ii).* Let `X` be the Fermat variety of dimension `2d`, `R = Z[t_1, …, t_{2d+1}]/(t_i^m − 1)`, and `K := 𝒥(s, d)`. A matching `J ∈ K` is `J = J' ⊔ {{2i, 2i+1} : s < i ≤ d}` with `J'` a matching of `{0, …, 2s+1}`, and `J' ↦ J` is a bijection from `𝒥` in dimension `2s` onto `K`. By §2,

`ψ_J = ψ_{J'} · Π_{i=s+1}^{d} g_i`,   `g_i := (t_{2i+1} − 1)·φ(t_{2i}t_{2i+1})`,

where `ψ_{J'}` is the generator of [DS] in dimension `2s`, a polynomial in `t_1, …, t_{2s+1}`. Let `p` be a prime and `F := F_p`. The ring `R ⊗ F` is the tensor product of `F[t_1, …, t_{2s+1}]/(t_i^m − 1)` and the rings `F[t_{2i}, t_{2i+1}]/(t_{2i}^m − 1, t_{2i+1}^m − 1)`, `i > s`. By Lemma 6.4 (which holds over any field), the ideal generated by the `ψ̄_J`, `J ∈ K`, is `I_{2s} ⊗ ⊗_{i>s} (ḡ_i)`, where `I_{2s}` is the ideal of Conjecture 1.2 in dimension `2s`, and its dimension is the product of the dimensions. In `F[t_{2i}, t_{2i+1}]/(t^m − 1)`, put `u := t_{2i}t_{2i+1}`; the ring is `F[u, t_{2i+1}]/(u^m − 1, t_{2i+1}^m − 1)`, and `ḡ_i = (t_{2i+1} − 1)·φ(u)`. The ideal `(φ(u))` of `F[u]/(u^m − 1)` is the line spanned by `φ(u)`, since `uφ(u) = φ(u)`; the ideal `(t − 1)` of `F[t]/(t^m − 1)` has dimension `m − 1`. By Lemma 6.4 again, `dim(ḡ_i) = m − 1`, over every field. Hence

`dim_{F_p}(ψ̄_J : J ∈ K) = dim_{F_p} I_{2s} · (m − 1)^{d−s}`.

By the Main Theorem in dimension `2s` and Proposition 2.1, `dim_{F_p} I_{2s} = |Γ_𝒥^{(2s)}| = Q_s(m)` for every prime `p` dividing `m` (for `s = 0`, `I_0 = (t_1 − 1)` has dimension `m − 1 = Q_0(m)` directly). On the other hand `|Γ_K| = Q_s(m)(m − 1)^{d−s}`: by [DS, Definition 1.3], a tuple `(a_1, …, a_{2d+1})` lies in `Γ_K` iff `(a_1, …, a_{2s+1}) ∈ Γ_𝒥^{(2s)}` and each pair `(a_{2i}, a_{2i+1})`, `i > s`, is `(a, a^{−1})` with `a ≠ 1`. So `dim_{F_p}(ψ̄_J : J ∈ K) = |Γ_K|` at every `p | m`, and by Proposition 2.1 (which holds for every non-empty `K ⊆ 𝒥`) `H_{2d}(X)/L_K(X)` is torsion free. Its rank is `|Γ_K| + 1` by Theorem 0(c). ∎

*Proof of Corollary W(i).* Consider the family of hypersurfaces `f_0(z_0, z_1) + ⋯ + f_d(z_{2d}, z_{2d+1}) = 0` with `f_0 = ⋯ = f_s = x^m + y^m` and `f_{s+1}, …, f_d` binary forms of degree `m` without repeated factors. By Euler's identity its members are smooth; its parameter space is the complement of a discriminant hypersurface in an affine space, hence connected; it contains `X` (all `f_i = x^m + y^m`) and `W_s`; and along it the `m` roots of each `f_i`, `i > s`, move continuously. The path must stay in this subfamily: the spaces `L_{J,β}` whose first `2s + 2` coordinates are paired by a matching other than `{0,1}, …, {2s, 2s+1}` involve `f_0, …, f_s` and need not deform if these forms move. By [DS, §4.6], along such a path from the Fermat variety `X` to `W_s`, the subspaces `L_{J,β}` with `J ∈ 𝒥(s, d)` deform to the `d`-spaces of `W_s`, and parallel transport (constant with respect to the Gauss–Manin connection) carries `L_{𝒥(s,d)}(X)` onto the subgroup of `H_{2d}(W_s; Z)` generated by these `d`-spaces; transport preserves rank and primitivity. So (i) follows from (ii), with the same rank `Q_s(m)(m−1)^{d−s} + 1`. ([DS, §4.6] reach the same point through the module `𝔐̄_{𝒥_s}(2d) = 𝔐̄_{𝒥(2s)}(2s) ⊗_Z S̄(s, d)` of their Theorem 1.1(d), which is free «if and only if Conjecture 1.2 holds for Fermat varieties of dimension `2s`».) ∎

*Remarks 11.1.* (1) We have read the argument of [DS, §4.6] in the original and made the choice of the path explicit, but we have not re-derived the transport statement (the continuity of the family of subspaces and the invariance of primitivity under parallel transport), on which Corollary W(i) depends; part (ii) is proved above from Theorem 0, Proposition 2.1, Lemma 6.4 and the Main Theorem. (2) The rank in (ii), `Q_s(m)(m − 1)^{d−s} + 1`, was also checked by enumerating `Γ_K` from [DS, Definition 1.3] at `m = 3, 5` and `d ≤ 3` (`14` cells; the enumeration is in the repository [Rep]). (3) We make no claim about the Hodge classes of `W_s`: its rational Hodge classes are not computed here, so Corollary W is a primitivity statement only.

## 13. Open problems

1. **Even degrees.** For `m = 2^v`, and more generally for even `m`, `2` is not a unit, the coordinate `y = t − t^{−1}` of Lemma 2.3 does not exist, the involution on `μ_m ∖ {1}` has a fixed point, and Theorem A fails in characteristic `2`. The colour reduction of §6 still splits off the odd part of `m`, but the block of the prime `2` needs a different method. A first step would be to compute the leading monomials of `(ψ̄_J)` at `(k, m) = (1, 4), (2, 4), (1, 8), (2, 8)`.
2. **The remaining integral Hodge classes.** When `m` is not prime and has a prime factor at most `2k + 1`, find generators of `Hdg(X)` beyond `L(X)`. By Corollary H(ii) the missing part is a free group of rank `|𝔅| − Q_k(m)` (Remark 10.3, Lemma 10.4), and the integral question is whether algebraic cycles fill it. For `k = 1` they do, by the Lefschetz `(1,1)` theorem; the question is for `k ≥ 2`. The rational Hodge conjecture for these `X` is known in many cases [Shi79], [Aok], [Jum].
3. **Elementary divisors.** For `m = p` prime, compute the elementary divisors and the discriminant of `Hdg(X) = L(X)` in closed form for `k ≥ 2`; [AMV, Table 1] gives the data for small cells. For `k = 1` the closed forms are the author's *Watermark* and *Double Ladder* theorems [WDL] (Remark 10.2). In every cell of [AMV, Table 1] with `m` prime, `|disc Hdg(X)|` is a power of `m`; whether this holds for every `k` is part of the question.
4. **A direct proof of Corollary 7.8.** Prove `dim_{F̄_p} I^{bal}_a ≤ N_{bal}(a, q)` on the bipartite block itself, without passing through the sum over the colourings of §6; since the dimension over `Q` is larger (Remark 7.9(2)), such a proof must use the characteristic. Equality `dim V_Λ = |Z_Λ|` for every down-set of Theorem 7.6, measured at every down-set we tested (§12.4) and never contradicted, would give it.
5. **Representation theory.** The Specht module route at `q = 3` (Remark 4.2(3)), and more generally a representation-theoretic description of the rings `C_m/V_Λ` (Complement to Theorem 5.9) and of the bipartite quotients.
6. **Theorem A for even `q`.** Prove that for every field of characteristic `≠ 2`, every even `q = 2m` and every `n`, `dim F[x_1, …, x_n]/(e_1, e_3, …; x_1^q, …, x_n^q)` is the number of walks of length `n` on `Z^m` with steps `±ε_i` from `0` to `0` (`n` even) or from `0` to `ε_1` (`n` odd). In characteristic `0` and for even `n` this follows from Kostant's theorem for `sp_{2m}`; the rest is computed in a few cells (Remark 8.4(4)). The proof of Theorem A uses the value `0` of the grid, which an even `q` does not have.

## Appendix A. Proof of Theorem A

This appendix is independent of §2–§5. **Its symbols are local:** here `L` is a finite set of variables, `A` and `B` are subsets of `L`, `C` is a set of field elements (Lemma A.4), `J` is an ideal (Proposition A.5), `R_a(G)` are the «rows» of Lemma A.6, `K_μ(L)` are ideals, and `Θ` is a generating function (it has nothing to do with `Γ_K` of [DS]).

### A.0 Conventions
- `F` is a field with `2 ∈ F^×`, `q ≥ 1` is odd, and `h = (q−1)/2`.
- For `X ⊆ L`, `E_X(t) := Π_{x∈X}(1 + xt)` and `e_j(X) := [t^j]E_X(t)`, so `e_0 = 1` and `e_j = 0` for `j < 0` or `j > |X|`. `I_q(L) := (e_j(L) : j odd) + box_L` with `box_L := (x^q : x ∈ L)`.
- `[t^D]` of a power series is `0` when `D < 0`.
- `I_d(2y) := Σ_{b≥0} y^{2b+d}/(b!(b+d)!)` for `d ≥ 0`, and `N_q(n) := n!·[y^n] e^y I_0(2y)^h`.
- A **partition** is a finite multiset of positive integers `μ_1 ≥ ⋯ ≥ μ_ℓ`, with `ℓ = ℓ(μ)` and `|μ| = Σμ_i`. For `w ≥ 0`, `β_w(μ) := Σ_i max(μ_i − w, 0)` (the number of boxes of the Young diagram to the right of column `w`), and `c_w(μ) := β_w(μ) − β_{w+1}(μ) = #{i : μ_i ≥ w+1}`. So `β_0(μ) = |μ|`, `c_0(μ) = ℓ(μ)`, and `c_w(μ) ≤ ℓ(μ)` for all `w`.

*Strategy.* Adjoin one variable `z` to `L`. Every ideal `G` of `F[L ∪ z]` containing `z^q` has `q` rows `R_0(G) ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ R_{q−1}(G)`, and `dim F[L∪z]/G = Σ_a dim F[L]/R_a(G)` (Lemma A.6). The count satisfies the matching recursion `N_μ(n+1) = Σ_a N_{child_a(μ)}(n)` (Lemma A.3), which is the derivative of a product of Bessel functions. So it suffices to attach to each `μ` an ideal `K_μ(L)` with `K_∅(L) = I_q(L)` such that every row of `K_μ(L ∪ z)` contains the ideal of the corresponding child (Theorem A.12). The ideals come from one generating function `Θ`, and adjoining `z` to one of three sets shifts an index of `Θ` in a controlled way (Lemma A.8). This is the same peeling idea as §5.1, with the box `q` in place of `q − 1`.

### A.1 The count and the dictionary

For a partition `μ` with `ℓ(μ) ≤ h`, put `F_μ(y) := e^y · Π_{i=1}^{ℓ} I_{μ_i}(2y) · I_0(2y)^{h−ℓ}` and `N_μ(n) := n!·[y^n]F_μ(y)`, so that `N_q(n) = N_∅(n)`.

**Lemma A.1.** *For `d ≥ 1`, `(d/dy) I_d(2y) = I_{d−1}(2y) + I_{d+1}(2y)`, and `(d/dy) I_0(2y) = 2 I_1(2y)`.*

*Proof.* Termwise, with `(2b+d) = b + (b+d)`:
`Σ_b (2b+d) y^{2b+d−1}/(b!(b+d)!) = Σ_{b≥1} y^{2b+d−1}/((b−1)!(b+d)!) + Σ_b y^{2b+d−1}/(b!(b+d−1)!)`.
The first sum is `I_{d+1}(2y)` (substitute `b ↦ b+1`). The second is `I_{d−1}(2y)` if `d ≥ 1`, and for `d = 0` it is again `I_1(2y)`. ∎

**Definition A.2 (the dictionary).** Let `ℓ = ℓ(μ) ≤ h`. For `a ∈ {0, …, q−1}`, `child_a(μ)` is the partition:
- **lower**, `0 ≤ a ≤ ℓ−1`: `μ` with one copy of `μ_{a+1}` replaced by `μ_{a+1} − 1`; parts are lowered **largest first**, and a part `0` is deleted;
- **value-0**, `a = ℓ`: `μ`;
- **new-class**, `ℓ+1 ≤ a ≤ q−ℓ−1`: `μ ∪ (1)`;
- **raise**, `a = q−j` with `1 ≤ j ≤ ℓ`: `μ` with one copy of `μ_j` replaced by `μ_j + 1`; row `q−1` raises the largest part.

New-class rows exist iff `ℓ+1 ≤ q−ℓ−1`, i.e. iff `ℓ + 1 ≤ h`. Hence every child has at most `h` parts.

**Lemma A.3.** *`N_μ(0) = [μ = ∅]`, and `N_μ(n+1) = Σ_{a=0}^{q−1} N_{child_a(μ)}(n)` for every `n ≥ 0`.*

*Proof.* The first claim holds because `F_μ(0) = [μ = ∅]`. For the second, `N_μ(n+1) = n!·[y^n]F_μ'`. By the product rule and Lemma A.1, `F_μ'` is a sum of:
- `F_μ`, from `e^y`;
- for each `i`, `F` of `μ` with `μ_i` replaced by `μ_i − 1`, plus `F` of `μ` with `μ_i` replaced by `μ_i + 1` (a part `0` is a factor `I_0`);
- `2(h−ℓ)` copies of `F_{μ∪(1)}`, from `I_0^{h−ℓ}`.

These are exactly the `ℓ + 1 + 2(h−ℓ) + ℓ = q` children of Definition A.2. ∎

**Lemma A.4.** *Let `C` be a set of `q` elements of a field of characteristic `≠ 2` with `0 ∈ C = −C`. Then `N_q(n)` is the number of `c ∈ C^n` such that `#{i : c_i = v} = #{i : c_i = −v}` for every `v ∈ C ∖ {0}`.*

*Proof.* `C ∖ {0}` splits into `h` pairs `{v, −v}` with `v ≠ −v`. By the exponential formula:
- the zero coordinates contribute the factor `e^y`;
- each pair with `b` coordinates equal to `v` and `b` equal to `−v` contributes `Σ_b y^{2b}/(b!·b!) = I_0(2y)`. ∎


### A.2 The lower bound

**Proposition A.5.** *`dim_F F[L]/I_q(L) ≥ N_q(|L|)`.*

*Proof.*

*Reduction to a large field.* Let `F'` be an infinite extension of `F`. The dimension is unchanged by extension of scalars, because `F'[L]/I_q(L)F'[L] = (F[L]/I_q(L)) ⊗_F F'`. Choose `c_1, …, c_h ∈ F'^×` with `c_i ≠ ±c_j` for `i ≠ j`, and put `C := {0, ±c_1, …, ±c_h}`. These are `q` distinct elements, since `c ≠ −c` for `c ≠ 0`.

*A radical ideal with the right count.* Let `f(x) := Π_{c∈C}(x − c)` and `J := (e_j(L) : j odd) + (f(x) : x ∈ L)`.
- By the Chinese remainder theorem, `F'[L]/(f(x) : x ∈ L) ≅ F'^{C^L}`, a product of copies of `F'`.
- Every quotient of a finite product of fields by an ideal is the product over a subset of the factors. Hence `F'[L]/J ≅ F'^Z`, where `Z` is the set of `c ∈ C^L` at which every odd `e_j` vanishes.
- For such `c`, all odd `e_j(c)` vanish iff `Π_x(1 + c_x t)` is an even polynomial, i.e. `Π_x(1+c_x t) = Π_x(1 − c_x t)`.
- By unique factorisation in `F'[t]`, this holds iff the multiset `{c_x ≠ 0}` is closed under negation.
- So `dim F'[L]/J = |Z| = N_q(n)` by Lemma A.4.

*Degeneration.* Let `in(J)` be the span of the top-degree forms of the elements of `J`.
- `(e_j : j odd) ⊆ in(J)`, since the `e_j` are homogeneous.
- `x^q ∈ in(J)`, since `x^q` is the top form of `f(x)`.

Hence `I_q(L) ⊆ in(J)`. For a degree-compatible monomial order, `LM(g) = LM(top(g))`. Moreover, for fixed `d` the non-zero top forms of degree-`d` elements of `J` form, with `0`, a vector space. So `LM(J) = LM(in(J))`, and by Macaulay's basis theorem `dim F'[L]/in(J) = dim F'[L]/J`. Therefore

`dim F'[L]/I_q(L) ≥ dim F'[L]/in(J) = N_q(n)`. ∎


### A.3 Rows

**Lemma A.6.** *Let `z ∉ L`, and let `G ⊆ F[L ∪ z]` be an ideal with `z^q ∈ G`. For `0 ≤ a ≤ q−1` put*

`R_a(G) := {f ∈ F[L] : z^a f ∈ G + z^{a+1}F[L∪z]}`.

*Then:*
- *each `R_a(G)` is an ideal of `F[L]`;*
- *`R_a(G) ⊆ R_{a+1}(G)`;*
- *`dim_F F[L∪z]/G = Σ_{a=0}^{q−1} dim_F F[L]/R_a(G)`, in `N ∪ {∞}`.*

*Proof.*
- **Ideal:** `R_a(G)` is an additive group, and for `g ∈ F[L]` we have `z^a gf = g·z^a f`.
- **Monotone:** multiplying `z^a f ∈ G + (z^{a+1})` by `z` gives `z^{a+1} f ∈ G + (z^{a+2})`.
- **Dimension:** put `G_a := G + z^aF[L∪z]`. Then `G_0 = F[L∪z]` and `G_q = G`. The map `F[L] → G_a/G_{a+1}`, `f ↦ z^a f`, is surjective because `z^aF[L∪z] = z^aF[L] + z^{a+1}F[L∪z]`, and its kernel is `R_a(G)`. Summing over `a` gives the formula. ∎


### A.4 The generating function

Fix disjoint `A, B ⊆ L`. Put:
- `P := L ∖ (A ∪ B)`;
- `r := |A|`;
- `w := |B| − |A|`, the **imbalance**.

For `a ∈ A` let `φ_a(t) := (1 − at)/(1 + at) = 1 + Σ_{i≥1} 2(−1)^i a^i t^i`.

**Definition A.7.** For `s ∈ Z`, let `D_L(A,B;s) := r(q−1) + |P| + 1 − s` and

`Θ^s_L(A;B) := [t^{D_L(A,B;s)}] E_P(−t)·Π_{a∈A} φ_a(t) ∈ F[L]`.

We call `s` the **level**.

*Remark.* With `H_{A;B}(t) := Π_{a∈A}(1+at)^{−1}Π_{b∈B}(1−bt)^{−1}` one has `E_L(−t)H_{A;B}(t) = E_P(−t)Π_{a∈A}φ_a(t)`: the factors of `B` cancel. So `B` enters `Θ` only through `P`, that is, through the degree `D`.

**Lemma A.8 (the three moves, and vanishing).** *Let `z ∉ L` and `L^+ := L ∪ {z}`. For disjoint `A, B ⊆ L` and `s ∈ Z`:*

- **(P)** `Θ^s_{L^+}(A;B) = Θ^{s−1}_L(A;B) − z·Θ^s_L(A;B)`;
- **(B)** `Θ^s_{L^+}(A;B∪{z}) = Θ^s_L(A;B)`;
- **(A)** `Θ^s_{L^+}(A∪{z};B) = Θ^{s−q+1}_L(A;B) + Σ_{i≥1} 2(−1)^i z^i·Θ^{s−q+1+i}_L(A;B)`, a finite sum;
- **(V)** *if `s ≤ 0`, then `Θ^s_L(A;B) ∈ box_A`, and `Θ^s_L(A;B) = 0` if `A = ∅`.*

*Proof.*
- **(P)** In `L^+`, `z` lies in the set `P^+ = P ∪ {z}`, and `E_{P^+}(−t) = E_P(−t)(1 − zt)`. Since `D_{L^+}(A,B;s) = D_L(A,B;s−1)` and `D_{L^+}(A,B;s) − 1 = D_L(A,B;s)`, the identity follows.
- **(B)** Neither `P` nor `D` changes.
- **(A)** One more factor `φ_z(t)`, and `D_{L^+}(A∪z,B;s) = D_L(A,B;s) + (q−1)`. The coefficient of `z^i t^i` in `φ_z` is `1` for `i = 0` and `2(−1)^i` for `i ≥ 1`. Finally, level `u` of `(A;B)` in `L` has degree `D_L(A,B;s) + (q−1) − i` exactly when `u = s − q + 1 + i`.
- **(V)** `Θ^s_L(A;B) = Σ_p (−1)^p e_p(P)·[t^{D−p}]Π_A φ_a`. The coefficient of `t^m` in `Π_A φ_a` is homogeneous of degree `m` in the `r` letters of `A`. For `s ≤ 0` every `p ≤ |P|` gives `D − p ≥ r(q−1)+1`. So every monomial has some exponent `≥ q` when `r ≥ 1`, and `[t^{D−p}]1 = 0` when `r = 0`. ∎

**Lemma A.9 (descent).** *For `a ≥ 0`:*

`z^a·Θ^s_L(A;B) = Θ^{s−a}_{L^+}(A;B∪{z}) − Σ_{i=1}^{a} z^{i−1}·Θ^{s−a+i}_{L^+}(A;B)`.

*Proof.* The case `a = 0` is (B). By (P) and (B), `z·Θ^s_L(A;B) = Θ^{s−1}_{L^+}(A;B∪z) − Θ^s_{L^+}(A;B)`, and applying (B) again to the first term gives `zΘ^s_L = Θ^{s−1}_L(A;B) − Θ^s_{L^+}(A;B)`. Hence

`z^aΘ^s_L = z^{a−1}Θ^{s−1}_L(A;B) − z^{a−1}Θ^s_{L^+}(A;B)`,

and the formula follows by induction on `a`. ∎

**Lemma A.10 (the odd identity).** *Let `|A| = |B| = r`, let `c ∈ Z`, `M := D_L(A,B;c)`, and `h_m := [t^m]H_{A;B}(t)`. For every `ℓ ≥ 0`,*

`z^{ℓ+1}Θ^{c+1}_L(A;B) ≡ Ω − ½·z^ℓ·((−1)^M Θ^c_L(B;A) − Θ^c_L(A;B))   mod z^{ℓ+2}F[L^+]`,

*where `Ω := z^ℓ Σ_{j odd} e_j(L^+)h_{M−j} − z^{ℓ+1}Σ_{j odd} e_j(L^+)h_{M−1−j}` lies in `(e_j(L^+) : j odd)`.*

*Proof.* Put `α_m := [t^m]E_L(t)H_{A;B}(t)` and `β_m := [t^m]E_L(−t)H_{A;B}(t)`. By definition and the Remark after Definition A.7, `β_M = Θ^c_L(A;B)` and `β_{M−1} = Θ^{c+1}_L(A;B)`.

Next, `E_L(t)H_{A;B}(t) = E_P(t)Π_B(1+bt)/(1−bt)`, which at `t = −u` is `E_L(−u)H_{B;A}(u)`. Since `|A| = |B|`, the degree `M` corresponds to the same level `c` for `(B;A)`, so `α_M = (−1)^M Θ^c_L(B;A)`.

Let `X_m := [t^m]O(t)H_{A;B}(t)` with `O(t) := Σ_{j odd} e_j(L^+)t^j = ½(E_{L^+}(t) − E_{L^+}(−t))`, so that `Ω = z^ℓX_M − z^{ℓ+1}X_{M−1}`. From `E_{L^+}(±t) = E_L(±t)(1 ± zt)`:

`X_m = ½(α_m + zα_{m−1} − β_m + zβ_{m−1})`.

Hence

`Ω = ½z^ℓ(α_M − β_M) + z^{ℓ+1}β_{M−1} − ½z^{ℓ+2}(α_{M−2} + β_{M−2})`.

Rearranging gives the claim. ∎


### A.5 The ideals

**Definition A.11.** For a partition `μ` with `ℓ(μ) ≤ h` and a finite set `L`,

`K_μ(L) := I_q(L) + ( Θ^s_L(A;B) : A, B ⊆ L disjoint, w = |B| − |A| ≥ 0, 1 ≤ s ≤ β_w(μ) )`.

*Remarks.*
1. `K_∅(L) = I_q(L)`, since `β_w(∅) = 0`.
2. If `μ ≠ ∅` and `L = ∅`, then `Θ^1_∅(∅;∅) = [t^0]1 = 1`, so `K_μ(∅) = F`.
3. For `A = ∅`, the generators are `±e_{|S|+1−s}(S)` with `S = L ∖ B`, allowed for `s ≤ β_{n−|S|}(μ)`. For `S ⊊ L` this is Tanisaki's rule for the partition `μ ∪ (1^{n−|μ|})` of `n` [Ta], [GP]; for `S = L` it adds the `e_j(L)` with `j ≥ n − |μ| + 1`.
4. The case `s = 1`, `A ≠ ∅` gives `±2^r (Π_A a^{q−1})·Π_P x` modulo `box`.
5. The generators with `A ≠ ∅` and higher `s` are not suggested by Tanisaki's rule. They were first found by computation, as the corrections needed for `μ = (1,1)`, `(1,1,1)` and `(2,2)`, and the generating function `Θ` organises them.


### A.6 The row inclusions

**Theorem A.12.** *Let `μ` be a partition with `ℓ := ℓ(μ) ≤ h`, `L` a finite set, `z ∉ L` and `K^+ := K_μ(L ∪ z)`. For every `a ∈ {0, …, q−1}`:*

`R_a(K^+) ⊇ K_{child_a(μ)}(L)`.

**Lemma A.13.** *Let `A, B ⊆ L` be disjoint with `w := |B|−|A| ≥ 0`.*

- **(i)** *If `s ≥ 1` and `s + 1 ≤ β_w(μ)`, then `Θ^s_L(A;B) ∈ R_0(K^+)`.*
- **(ii)** *If `s ∈ Z`, `s ≤ β_w(μ)`, `a ≥ 0` and `s − a ≤ β_{w+1}(μ)`, then `z^a·Θ^s_L(A;B) ∈ K^+`. (For `s ≤ 0` this holds already by (V).)*
- **(iii)** *`I_q(L) ⊆ R_0(K^+)`.*

*Proof.*
- **(i)** `Θ^{s+1}_{L^+}(A;B)` is a generator of `K^+`: its imbalance is `w` and its level `s+1 ≤ β_w(μ)`. By (P) it equals `Θ^s_L(A;B) − zΘ^{s+1}_L(A;B)`.
- **(ii)** Use Lemma A.9.
  - The first term has imbalance `w+1` and level `s − a ≤ β_{w+1}(μ)`.
  - The terms of the sum have imbalance `w` and levels in `[s−a+1, s] ⊆ (−∞, β_w(μ)]`.
  - Each term is a generator of `K^+` if its level is `≥ 1`, and lies in `box` by (V) otherwise.
- **(iii)** `e_j(L) = e_j(L^+) − z·e_{j−1}(L)`, and `x^q ∈ K^+` for `x ∈ L`. ∎

*Proof of Theorem A.12.* By Lemma A.6 the rows increase, so it suffices to place every generator of `K_{child_a(μ)}(L)` in the row `a` assigned by the dictionary (or in an earlier row). Generators from `I_q(L)` are handled by (iii). So consider a child generator `Θ^s_L(A;B)` with `w = |B|−|A| ≥ 0` and `1 ≤ s ≤ β_w(child)`. Recall `β_w − β_{w+1} = c_w ≤ ℓ`.

**Lower, `0 ≤ a ≤ ℓ−1`.** Here `β_w(child) = β_w(μ) − [μ_{a+1} > w]`.
- If `μ_{a+1} > w`, then `s + 1 ≤ β_w(μ)`, and (i) applies.
- If `μ_{a+1} ≤ w`, then all parts `> w` are among `μ_1, …, μ_a` (parts are ordered decreasingly), so `c_w(μ) ≤ a`. Hence `s − a ≤ β_w(μ) − c_w(μ) = β_{w+1}(μ)`, and (ii) gives `z^aΘ^s_L ∈ K^+`.

**Value-0, `a = ℓ`.** Here `s ≤ β_w(μ)` and `s − ℓ ≤ β_w(μ) − c_w(μ) = β_{w+1}(μ)`, so (ii) applies.

**New-class, `ℓ+1 ≤ a ≤ q−ℓ−1`.** Here `β_w(μ ∪ (1)) = β_w(μ) + [w = 0]`.
- Every generator other than `(w, s) = (0, |μ|+1)` is a generator of `K_μ(L)`, hence lies in row `ℓ ⊆` row `a` by the value-0 case.
- For `w = 0` (so `|A| = |B|`) and `s = |μ| + 1`, apply Lemma A.10 with `c = |μ|`:
  - `Ω ∈ K^+`;
  - by (ii) with `a = ℓ`, both `z^ℓΘ^{|μ|}_L(A;B)` and `z^ℓΘ^{|μ|}_L(B;A)` lie in `K^+`. The pairs `(A;B)` and `(B;A)` have imbalance `0`; `|μ| = β_0(μ)`; and `|μ| − ℓ = β_0 − c_0 = β_1(μ)`.
  - Hence `z^{ℓ+1}Θ^{|μ|+1}_L(A;B) ∈ K^+ + (z^{ℓ+2})`, i.e. the generator lies in `R_{ℓ+1} ⊆ R_a`.

**Raise, `a = q − j`, `1 ≤ j ≤ ℓ`.** Here `β_w(child) = β_w(μ) + [μ_j ≥ w]`, and `q − j ≥ q − ℓ ≥ ℓ + 1` because `2ℓ ≤ q−1`.
- *Old levels*, `s ≤ β_w(μ)`: `s − (q−j) ≤ β_w(μ) − ℓ − 1 < β_{w+1}(μ)`, so (ii) applies.
- *New level at `w = 0`*, `s = |μ| + 1`: the new-class argument places it in `R_{ℓ+1} ⊆ R_{q−j}`.
- *New level at `w ≥ 1`* (only when `μ_j ≥ w`): `s = t := β_w(μ) + 1`. Put `s' := β_{w−1}(μ)` and consider `Π := Θ^{s'}_{L^+}(A ∪ {z}; B)`.
  - `Π` has imbalance `w − 1 ≥ 0` and level `s' ≥ c_{w−1}(μ) ≥ j ≥ 1`, so it is a generator of `K^+`.
  - By (A), `Π = Σ_{i≥0} κ_i z^i Θ^{u_i}_L(A;B)`, with `u_i := s' − q + 1 + i`, `κ_0 = 1` and `κ_i = 2(−1)^i` for `i ≥ 1`, all units.
  - Put `i* := q − c_{w−1}(μ)`. Then `u_{i*} = β_w(μ) + 1 = t`, and `i* ≤ q − j` because `μ_1, …, μ_j ≥ w` gives `c_{w−1}(μ) ≥ j`.
  - For `i < i*`: `u_i ≤ β_w(μ)`, and `u_i − i = s' − q + 1 = β_{w+1}(μ) + c_w(μ) + c_{w−1}(μ) − (q−1) ≤ β_{w+1}(μ)`, since `c_w + c_{w−1} ≤ 2ℓ ≤ q−1`. So `z^i Θ^{u_i}_L(A;B) ∈ K^+` by (ii), or lies in `box` if `u_i ≤ 0`. (This includes `i = 0`, which matters when `s' ≥ q`.)
  - Therefore `κ_{i*} z^{i*}Θ^t_L(A;B) ∈ K^+ + (z^{i*+1})`. Since `κ_{i*}` is a unit, `Θ^t_L(A;B) ∈ R_{i*} ⊆ R_{q−j}`. ∎

*Remark A.14 (the order of the lower rows is used).* The lower case uses «largest part first». With the opposite order, the inclusion fails already for `μ = (3,1)`, `q = 9`, `|L| = 5`: 65 generators of the child `(3)` lie outside row 0. The count of Lemma A.3 does not see the order; the inclusions do.


### A.7 Proof of Theorem A

**Theorem A.15.** *For every `n ≥ 0`, every set `L` of `n` variables and every partition `μ` with `ℓ(μ) ≤ h`: `dim_F F[L]/K_μ(L) ≤ N_μ(n)`.*

*Proof.* Induction on `n`.

*Base, `n = 0`.* `F[∅] = F`. For `μ = ∅`, `K_∅(∅) = 0` and `N_∅(0) = 1`. For `μ ≠ ∅`, `K_μ(∅) = F` (Remark 2 after Definition A.11) and `N_μ(0) = 0`.

*Step.* Let `|L| = n` and `z ∉ L`. Then `z^q ∈ K_μ(L∪z)`, and:

`dim F[L∪z]/K_μ(L∪z) = Σ_{a=0}^{q−1} dim F[L]/R_a(K_μ(L∪z))`   (Lemma A.6)
`≤ Σ_a dim F[L]/K_{child_a(μ)}(L)`   (Theorem A.12)
`≤ Σ_a N_{child_a(μ)}(n)`   (induction; every child has `≤ h` parts)
`= N_μ(n+1)`   (Lemma A.3). ∎

*Proof of Theorem A.* Take `μ = ∅` in Theorem A.15: `K_∅(L) = I_q(L)`, so `dim F[L]/I_q(L) ≤ N_q(n)`. Combine with Proposition A.5. ∎


### A.8 Proofs of Corollaries 8.1 and 8.2

*Corollary 8.1.* Take `F = F_p`, `q = p^v`, `n = 2k+2`. Then `A_k(q) = N_q(2k+2)` by Theorem A. Also `N_q(2k+2) = P_k(q)`: in the proof of Proposition A.5 with `F' = F̄_p` and the set of values `C = F_q ⊂ F'`, the set `Z` is `{x ∈ F_q^{2k+2} : e_j(x) = 0, j odd}`. ∎

*Corollary 8.2.* `M` is spanned by the monomials with exponents `< q`, so it is finitely generated. Write `M ≅ Z^ρ ⊕ T` with `T` finite. For a field `F`, `M ⊗ F ≅ F[L]/I_q(L)`, by right exactness of `⊗`. So:
- `ρ = dim_Q = N_q(n)`;
- for an odd prime `p`, `dim_{F_p} = ρ + (number of cyclic `p`-primary summands of `T`) = N_q(n)`.

Hence `T` has no odd primary part. ∎

*Remark A.16.* In characteristic `2` the statement fails, and the proof uses `½` in exactly one place (Lemma A.10). The measured dimensions at `n = 4` are `21, 65, 133, 225` for `q = 3, 5, 7, 9`, against `N_q(4) = 19, 61, 127, 217`.

## Appendix B. Theorem 0, modulo Pham's theorem and the intersection numbers

This appendix proves Theorem 0 for `n = 2k ≥ 2`, `m ≥ 3` and `∅ ≠ K ⊆ 𝒥`. It follows [DS, §4] closely, with every step written out. We use, without proof:
- **(T1) Classical topology [Mil, §7].** `H_*(X)` and `H_*(Y_0)` are torsion free, where `Y_0 := X ∩ {z_0 = 0}` is the Fermat hypersurface of dimension `n − 1` in `P^n`; the intersection form `⟨ , ⟩` on `H_n(X)` is unimodular; and by the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem `H_j(Y_0) → H_j(P^n)` is an isomorphism for `j < n − 1`. We also use three standard facts: the Gysin map `H_n(X) → H_{n−2}(Y_0)` sends the class of a cycle meeting `Y_0` transversally to the class of the intersection; two complex submanifolds of complementary dimension meeting transversally in one point have intersection number `+1`; and the action of `G` by holomorphic automorphisms preserves the intersection form and is compatible with Poincaré–Lefschetz duality [DS, §2].
- **(T2) Pham's theorem [Ph], [DS, Theorem 2.1].** Let `S` be the Pham cycle of [DS, §2], an `n`-sphere in `X ∖ Y_0`, and let `G` act on `H_n(X ∖ Y_0)`. The map `R → H_n(X ∖ Y_0)`, `1 ↦ [S]`, is surjective.
- **(T3) The intersection numbers [DS, Theorem 2.2].** Fix `η := e^{πi/m}`, and put `s(η) := 1`, `s(η^{−1}) := −1`, and `s(β) := 0` for the other `m`-th roots `β` of `−1`. Then `⟨L_{J,β}, S⟩ = sgn(σ_J)·Π_{i=0}^{k} s(β_i)`, where `σ_J` is the permutation `(0, 1, …, n+1) ↦ (j_0, k_0, …, j_k, k_k)`.

The group `G`, generated by `γ_i : z_i ↦ ζz_i` (`ζ := e^{2πi/m}`, `i = 1, …, n+1`), acts on `X` by holomorphic automorphisms; we identify `R` with its group ring through `t_i ↦ γ_i`. For an `R`-module `M` put `M^∨ := Hom_Z(M, Z)` with the contragredient action `(g·φ)(x) = φ(g^{−1}x)`.

**Step 1 (the Pham image is primitive, of corank 1).** Let `V := Im(H_n(X ∖ Y_0) → H_n(X))`. The cohomology sequence of the pair `(X, Y_0)`, with Poincaré–Lefschetz duality, gives an exact sequence of `R`-modules `H_n(X ∖ Y_0) → H_n(X) → H_{n−2}(Y_0)` [DS, §2]. The second map is the Gysin map, which sends the class of a cycle meeting `Y_0` transversally to the class of the intersection. By (T1), `H_{n−2}(Y_0) ≅ H_{n−2}(P^n) ≅ Z` (note `n − 2 < n − 1`), so `H_n(X)/V` embeds in `Z`: `V` is primitive. The map `H_n(X) → Z` is non-zero: a standard space `L_{J,β}` meets `Y_0` transversally in the linear `(k−1)`-space `L_{J,β} ∩ {z_0 = 0}` (on `L_{J,β}`, `z_{k_0} = β_0 z_0`, and `z_0` is a non-zero linear form). The intersection is transverse: at a point `x`, with `H := {z_0 = 0}`, `T_xY_0 = T_xX ∩ T_xH`, so `T_xL_{J,β} ∩ T_xY_0 = T_xL_{J,β} ∩ T_xH`, of dimension `k − 1`. Its class generates `H_{n−2}(Y_0)`, since its image in `H_{n−2}(P^n)` is the class of a linear `(k−1)`-space. Hence `rank V = rank H_n(X) − 1`. By (T2), `R → V`, `1 ↦ [S]`, is surjective.

**Step 2 (duals of surjections).** On `R` put `[t^ν, t^{ν'}] := δ_{ν,ν'}` (`ν, ν' ∈ (Z/m)^{n+1}`). This form is unimodular and `G`-invariant, so `x ↦ [x, ·]` is an isomorphism `R ≅ R^∨` of `R`-modules. Let `f : R → M` be a *surjective* homomorphism of `R`-modules. Then `f^∨ : M^∨ → R^∨ ≅ R` is injective, its image is the ideal `Ker(f)^⊥ := {x ∈ R : [x, y] = 0 for all y ∈ Ker f}`, and `R/Ker(f)^⊥` is torsion free.[^2] Indeed, an element of the image vanishes on `Ker f`; conversely, if `x ∈ Ker(f)^⊥`, then `[x, ·]` vanishes on `Ker f` and factors through `R/Ker f ≅ M`, so it lies in the image. Finally, if `c x ∈ Ker(f)^⊥` with `0 ≠ c ∈ Z`, then `c[x, y] = 0` for `y ∈ Ker f`, so `x ∈ Ker(f)^⊥`.

[^2]: As stated in [DS, §4.2], this description of the image of `f^∨` requires the additional hypothesis that `M/Im f` be torsion free (for instance, `f` surjective); this hypothesis holds in the case where [DS] apply it, the surjection of Step 1, so the result we use is not affected.

Apply this to the surjection `f : R → V` of Step 1. We obtain an ideal `V^∨ ⊆ R` with `R/V^∨` torsion free. Explicitly, the composite `H_n(X) ≅ H_n(X)^∨ → V^∨ ⊆ R`, where the first map is the (`G`-invariant) intersection form and the second is restriction, sends `τ` to the element `r_τ` with `[r_τ, t^ν] = ⟨τ, f(t^ν)⟩`, that is

`r_τ = Σ_{ν ∈ (Z/m)^{n+1}} ⟨τ, γ^ν[S]⟩ t^ν`.

**Step 3 (the kernel is generated by the class of a linear section, which lies in `L_K`).** The restriction `H_n(X)^∨ → V^∨` is surjective, because `V` is a direct summand of `H_n(X)` (a primitive subgroup of a free group); so `H_n(X) → V^∨` is surjective with kernel `V^⊥` (orthogonal for `⟨ , ⟩`), which is primitive of rank `1`. Let `P_X := [X ∩ Λ]` for a linear subspace `Λ ⊂ P^{n+1}` of dimension `k + 1` meeting `X` properly; this class does not depend on `Λ`, since `[X ∩ Λ] = h^k ∩ [X]` whenever the intersection is proper. Taking `Λ ⊂ {z_0 = 0}` shows `P_X ⊥ V`, since every class of `V` is carried by a cycle in `X ∖ Y_0`. And `⟨P_X, [L]⟩ = 1` for a standard space `L`, since `P_X` is the `k`-th power of the hyperplane class cut on `X` and `L` is a linear space of dimension `k`. So `P_X` is primitive and `V^⊥ = Z·P_X`. Now fix `J ∈ K` and `β` with `β^m = −1`, and let `Λ_J := {z_{k_i} = β z_{j_i} : i = 1, …, k}`, of dimension `k + 1`. On `Λ_J` one has `z_{j_i}^m + z_{k_i}^m = 0` for `i ≥ 1`, so `X ∩ Λ_J = {z_0^m + z_{k_0}^m = 0} ∩ Λ_J`, the reduced union of the `m` standard spaces `L_{J,(β_0, β, …, β)}`, `β_0^m = −1`. Hence `P_X ∈ L_K(X)`, and therefore

`H_n(X)/L_K(X) ≅ V^∨/L'_K`,   `rank L_K(X) = rank L'_K + 1`,

where `L'_K ⊆ V^∨ ⊆ R` is the image of `L_K(X)`. Since the maps are `R`-linear and `L_K(X)` is an `R`-submodule, `L'_K` is an ideal of `R`.

**Step 4 (the image of `L_{J}` is generated by `ψ_J`).** For `ν ∈ (Z/m)^{n+1}` write `γ^ν := Π γ_i^{ν_i}` and put `ν_0 := 0`. Since `γ^ν` multiplies `z_i` by `ζ^{ν_i}`, one has `(γ^ν)^{−1}(L_{J,β}) = L_{J,β'}` with `β'_i = ζ^{ν_{j_i} − ν_{k_i}}β_i`. For fixed `J` these translates exhaust all `β` (choose `ν_{j_i} = 0` and adjust `ν_{k_i}`), so `L_{{J}}(X)` is the `R`-module generated by `[L_{J,η}]`, `η := (η, …, η)`. By Step 2 and (T3), its image is `ψ'_J := Σ_ν a_ν t^ν` with

`a_ν = ⟨L_{J,η}, γ^ν[S]⟩ = ⟨(γ^ν)^{−1}L_{J,η}, S⟩ = sgn(σ_J)·Π_{i=0}^{k} e(ν_{k_i} − ν_{j_i})`,

where `e(c) := s(ζ^{−c}η)`, that is `e(0) = 1`, `e(1) = −1` (because `ζ^{−1}η = η^{−1}`), and `e(c) = 0` otherwise. The indices `k_0` and `j_i, k_i` (`i ≥ 1`) are `1, …, n+1`, each once, so the sum factorises, and `Σ_c e(c)t^c = 1 − t` and `Σ_{c,c'} e(c' − c)t_j^c t_k^{c'} = (1 − t_k)Σ_c (t_jt_k)^c = (1 − t_k)φ(t_jt_k)` give

`ψ'_J = sgn(σ_J)·(1 − t_{k_0})·Π_{i=1}^{k}(1 − t_{k_i})φ(t_{j_i}t_{k_i}) = ±ψ_J`.

Hence `L'_K = (ψ_J : J ∈ K)`.

**Step 5 (torsion transfer): Theorem 0(a).** The exact sequence `0 → V^∨/L'_K → R/L'_K → R/V^∨ → 0` has a torsion-free cokernel, so every torsion element of `R/L'_K` lies in `V^∨/L'_K`, and the torsion subgroups of `V^∨/L'_K` and `R/L'_K = A_K` coincide. With Step 3, `Tors(H_n(X)/L_K(X)) ≅ Tors(A_K)`.

**Step 6: Theorem 0(b)–(d).** (b) If `char K_p = p` with `p = 0` or `p ∤ m`, then `t^m − 1` is separable over `K_p`, so `R ⊗ K_p ≅ K_p^{μ_m^{n+1}}` is the ring of functions on `μ_m^{n+1}`, and `A_K ⊗ K_p` is the ring of functions on the common zeros of the `ψ_J`, `J ∈ K`. For `b ∈ μ_m`, `φ(b) = m ≠ 0` if `b = 1` and `φ(b) = 0` otherwise. So `ψ_J(a) ≠ 0` iff `a_{k_i} ≠ 1` for all `i` and `a_{j_i}a_{k_i} = 1` for `i ≥ 1`; since then `a_{j_i} = a_{k_i}^{−1} ≠ 1`, this says exactly `a ∈ Γ_J`. Hence `dim(A_K ⊗ K_p) = m^{n+1} − |Γ_K|`. (c) By Step 3 and (b) with `p = 0`, `rank L_K(X) = rank L'_K + 1 = (m^{n+1} − rank A_K) + 1 = |Γ_K| + 1`. (d) For `p ∤ m`, (b) with `K_p = F̄_p` gives `dim_{F_p}(A_K ⊗ F_p) = rank A_K`, so `A_K` has no `p`-torsion, and by (a) neither has `H_n(X)/L_K(X)`. ∎
