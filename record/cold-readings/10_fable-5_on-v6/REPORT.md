# 6. THE WEIGHT OF THE THEOREM AND WHERE IT DESERVES TO BE PUBLISHED

**Significance.** Conjecture 1.2 of Degtyarev–Shimada (2016) is a natural, precise question about a classical object: whether the lattice spanned by the standard linear subspaces of the Fermat variety is primitive in the middle homology. It had been proved only for surfaces (Degtyarev) and checked by computer in a handful of cells. The paper settles it for every odd degree in every even dimension. Because the *rational* Hodge classes of prime-degree Fermat varieties were known to be spanned by linear subspaces (Shioda, Ran) and Aoki determined exactly when every Hodge character is of pair type, the primitivity upgrades those classical statements to the *integral* Hodge conjecture, in the strong form `Hdg(X) = L(X)`, for prime degrees and for degrees prime to `(n+2)!`, and gives a clean description (`L(X)` primitive with a free complement of computable rank) in the remaining odd degrees. That is a real result in a field where integral Hodge statements are scarce and mostly negative (Atiyah–Hirzebruch, Kollár) or hard-won (Voisin for cubic fourfolds); the Fermat family is the standard test bed. Two honest limitations: the even degrees remain open, so the conjecture is not fully resolved; and the rational part of the picture was classical, so the novelty is the integral/lattice-theoretic layer, not a new Hodge-theoretic phenomenon.

**Main contribution: theorem and method, in that order.** The theorem is the headline. The method — the down-set induction over partitions under weak dominance, with divided differences and Vandermonde determinants as the only glue, and its bipartite extension — is new to me and is more than a device: the Complement to Theorem 5.9 identifies the quotients as associated graded rings of coordinate rings of explicit point sets, a Garsia–Procesi/Tanisaki-type statement for a new family. Whether it has uses beyond this problem is unproven, and the proofs are long case analyses rather than conceptual arguments, which limits the method's immediate appeal. Corollary W is a welcome but incremental removal of a hypothesis in [DS]. Theorem A is a separate, moderately interesting count (its `q = 3` case was known through tilting theory) and does not add weight to the paper; it subtracts focus.

**Venue, in tiers (general knowledge of the journals' scope and selectivity; I have not checked current editorial policies, in particular on AI-assisted manuscripts, and the author should).**
- *Tier 1 (Annals, Inventiones, JAMS, Acta):* not warranted — a specific conjecture in a classical family, with the even case open, is below the reach these journals expect.
- *Tier 2 (Duke, Compositio, Advances in Mathematics, Crelle, Algebra & Number Theory, Mathematische Annalen):* a stretch but not absurd for a complete resolution of a published conjecture with a new method and a strong Hodge-theoretic corollary; the length, the case analyses and the off-path material would count against it, and a referee there would demand the conceptual version of §7 (item G above). Algebra & Number Theory or Mathematische Annalen (where Aoki's and Shioda's Fermat papers appeared) are the plausible targets in this tier.
- *Tier 3 (Journal of the Mathematical Society of Japan, Mathematische Zeitschrift, Transactions of the AMS, IMRN, Journal of Algebraic Geometry, Journal of Algebra):* the natural home. **My choice: the Journal of the Mathematical Society of Japan**, where Degtyarev–Shimada posed the conjecture — the audience that cares most and can referee it best — with Mathematische Zeitschrift or Transactions of the AMS as alternatives; Journal of Algebra or Algebraic Combinatorics would suit a separate paper on the down-set theorems alone.

**What a referee there would most likely object to.** (1) Length and structure: Theorem A, §8, most of §9 and most of §12 are not on the path and should go; (2) the case analyses of Propositions 5.6 and 7.4 — correct, but the referee will ask for a more conceptual argument or at least a tabulated one; (3) the dependency description (E1) and the qualifier on Corollary W(i) (G1); (4) the provenance: extensive AI authorship without any human reading — the referee will either re-derive everything (as I did, and it holds) or decline; (5) the even-degree boundary — why the method stops at `p = 2`; (6) the verification narrative, which reads as a lab notebook and should become a supplement. None of these is a mathematical objection; all are addressable.
